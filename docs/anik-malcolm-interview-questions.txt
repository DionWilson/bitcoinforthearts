Share Your Bitcoin Journey: Anik Malcolm — Interview Questions

For BFTA Podcast Series · YouTube · Recording date TBD
Show host: Dion Wilson · Founder, Executive Director, Interim Chair, Bitcoin for the Arts

Source material reviewed: anikmalcolm.com · Bitcoin Magazine "The Whole Entire Universe:
21 Million, One Painting" · Bitcoin Art Podcast (Asanoha) Episode 4 · Lugano Plan B
exhibition page · BFTA Issue #6 newsletter feature.

Note for the host: Anik has already given his "The Whole Entire Universe" origin story
on the Asanoha podcast. This question set is calibrated to push past the standard
biographical beats and into territory those other interviews didn't fully cover —
specifically the establishment-rejection trajectory, his wife Una's role as creative
partner, the philosophical weight of choosing oil paint as a medium for Bitcoin,
the geography of where his work is being collected (Lugano, Vegas, Roatán — not
the traditional museum circuit), and fatherhood / generational sound money. The
questions are sequenced so the early ones warm up familiar ground briefly and the
middle questions hit angles he hasn't aired before publicly.


BACKGROUND & JOURNEY

1. Anik, welcome to Share Your Bitcoin Journey. You're a conceptual multimedia artist
   classically trained in London, who eventually left both London and Reykjavík out
   of frustration with the art establishment, and now lives with your wife Una and
   your children in the mountains of central Italy. For anyone meeting you for the
   first time — take us back to the beginning. When did you know you were going to
   spend your life making things, and what did "making things" mean to you as a kid?

2. Classical training in London is a particular kind of forge — there's a craft
   discipline there that not many artists go through anymore. What did the London
   training give you that you still draw on every day, even now that you've broken
   with that world?

3. You've described becoming disillusioned with the establishment in both London and
   later Reykjavík. Disillusionment is usually a moment, not a slow fade. Was there
   a specific encounter, exhibition, or conversation that broke the spell for you?

4. After London came Reykjavík — Iceland is a fascinating choice. Geothermal energy,
   a small but serious Bitcoin community, a creative tradition that's completely
   different from London's. Why Iceland? And what did being in Reykjavík do to your
   thinking that London couldn't have done?

5. And then central Italy — the mountains, with your wife and children. That's a
   meaningful relocation. There's a long European tradition of artists withdrawing
   from cities to do their most serious work. What did the move to Italy unlock for
   you that the cities couldn't?


THE BITCOIN TURN

6. Before Bitcoin clicked for you, you were already doing music and urban art
   projects — you'd been searching for the channel that would hold everything you
   were feeling about the status quo. How did you first encounter Bitcoin, and at
   what point did you realize this wasn't going to be a passing interest but the
   subject of the rest of your artistic life?

7. On your website you describe Bitcoin as having "given purpose to both [your] art
   and [your] life in general." That's a strong claim. A lot of Bitcoiners feel it.
   But to put it at the center of an entire career — that's commitment few artists
   make to any single subject. What gave you the conviction to fold your whole
   artistic identity into one theme?

8. Many artists who reference Bitcoin make it a small element — a laser eye here, a
   Satoshi quote there. You've gone the other direction completely. The tagline on
   your site is "depicting Bitcoin." Two words. What do you say to other artists
   who are afraid of committing that hard to a single subject?


THE WHOLE ENTIRE UNIVERSE

9. Let's talk about The Whole Entire Universe. The painting that debuted at
   Bitcoin 2026 at The Venetian. The math is genuinely beautiful — a 276 by 276
   by 276 cube of beads, which gives you 21,024,576 beads, then you remove 4,096
   beads from each of the six sides. 4,096 is a perfect square — 64 by 64 — and a
   power of two, so each removed area halves repeatedly down to 2 by 2, mirroring
   Bitcoin's halving mechanism. When did you discover that the math worked out that
   way? Was it something you architected, or did the universe present itself to you?

10. Your wife Una — who is also an artist and a jeweler — was the one who suggested
    representing the 21 million as beads, because beads were among the very first
    forms of currency human beings used. Talk to us about Una and that moment.
    What's it like making a piece this consequential where the conceptual seed came
    from your partner?

11. You and Una are both artists. You're raising children together in the mountains
    of central Italy, both making work, both thinking about money and meaning. How
    does the household function as a creative unit? Does the work bleed between you,
    or do you keep your practices separate?

12. The oil painting took you 900 hours. That number alone is a kind of statement —
    low time preference made literal. The most "Bitcoin" thing about the painting
    may not be its subject; it may be that you sat with it for 900 hours. Was that
    deliberate? Did you feel the parallel as you were painting?

13. The medium choice fascinates me. Bitcoin imagery has been dominated by glossy
    digital aesthetics — vector laser eyes, neon, futuristic typography. You went
    the other direction completely — oil paint, classical technique, a physical
    object that exists in real space. Why oil? Why classical technique for a subject
    that exists in code?

14. Beads as the conceptual unit — Una's idea — point back to the very origin of
    money. Your work is asking what money IS, not just what Bitcoin is. Do you see
    The Whole Entire Universe as a piece about Bitcoin, or as a piece about money
    itself, with Bitcoin happening to be the final form?


GEOGRAPHY OF A NEW PATRONAGE

15. Look at where your work has been shown and where it's headed: Lugano Plan B —
    a Swiss city that's actively embracing Bitcoin. The Venetian in Las Vegas for
    Bitcoin 2026 — the largest Bitcoin conference in the world. A planned monumental
    public sculpture in Roatán — an offshore Bitcoin community. That's a very
    specific map. You're being collected and exhibited in the emerging citadels of
    Bitcoin, not in the traditional museum circuit. Was that a conscious strategy,
    or did the work pull you there?

16. The Roatán public sculpture is the next major project. What can you tell us
    about what you're planning? How does scale change the meaning when the work
    leaves the gallery and becomes part of a place?

17. There's a real question underneath all of this — whether the next renaissance
    in serious craft is going to happen inside or outside the traditional art
    establishment. You've explicitly chosen outside. What does a renaissance look
    like that's being built in Lugano, in Roatán, at conferences, in artists'
    studios in mountain villages? What can it produce that MoMA can't?


FATHERHOOD AND THE LONG TIME HORIZON

18. You have children. You're making art that depicts a hard-money system that's
    been designed with your kids' generation specifically in mind. How does
    fatherhood inform what you're putting on the canvas? When you imagine your kids
    seeing The Whole Entire Universe twenty years from now, what do you want them
    to understand about why you made it?

19. Bitcoiners talk about "generational wealth" and "stack for your grandkids"
    constantly. But Bitcoin is also about generational MEANING — passing down a
    monetary culture, not just money. As both an artist and a father, what's the
    cultural inheritance you're trying to build?


THE FORWARD QUESTION

20. What's the next major work after the Roatán sculpture? Where is your practice
    heading over the next five to ten years?


THE CLOSING QUESTION

21. Last question — we ask every guest this on Share Your Bitcoin Journey:
    if a $500 to $2,000 Bitcoin micro-grant from Bitcoin for the Arts landed in
    your wallet tomorrow, what would it unlock for you as a creator?

NOTES ON ORDER

  - Q1-5 are biographical warmup. Don't dwell — get to Q6 within ~10 minutes.
  - Q6-8 are the Bitcoin turn. These should feel reflective; don't rush.
  - Q9-14 are the deep dive on The Whole Entire Universe. This is the heart of
    the episode. Let him talk. Follow tangents he opens up.
  - Q15-17 are the cultural commentary — where his work is being shown and
    what that says about the new patronage geography. This is where the
    episode earns its place in the BFTA series specifically.
  - Q18-19 are the human / generational layer. These often produce the
    most emotionally resonant moments. Don't skip if you're running long;
    instead, trim Q15-17 to make room.
  - Q20-21 are the wrap. Q21 is sacred — every episode ends with it.

If the conversation is flowing and you're running over 60 minutes, the
questions to consider cutting are: Q3 (disillusionment moment — touchy and
may have been covered with Asanoha), Q11 (household creative unit — nice
but optional if Q10 already opened that ground), and Q17 (renaissance —
worth a follow-up episode of its own if it doesn't land).
