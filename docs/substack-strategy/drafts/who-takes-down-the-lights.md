## BODY

At about 2:30 in the morning on Wednesday, November 18, 2026, a bargaining session at the Manhattan offices of Proskauer, the Broadway League's longtime law firm, ended without a deal.

On one side of the table: IATSE Local One, the union of stagehands who build, install, maintain, and run the lighting, sound, scenery, and special effects of Broadway. On the other: The Broadway League, which represents Broadway's producers and theater owners, and the Nederlander Organization, which controls nine Broadway theaters. IATSE International President Matthew Loeb was in the room. So was League President Jason Laks.

The stagehands' last contract, covering 32 of Broadway's 41 theaters, expired on July 26. Talks have run since September. On October 18, Local One members voted unanimously to authorize a strike. The week of November 16, Loeb authorized the local to call one if necessary. No date has been set. More sessions are scheduled for November 23 and 24.

Two days later, the Theatrical Wardrobe Union, IATSE Local 764, announced its own strike authorization vote for Sunday, November 22. Its members dress the performers, maintain the costumes, and execute every quick change in every show. They have been bargaining since June and working without a contract since July 26. The union says the League cancelled the meetings set for November 18 and 19.

Broadway has been here before. In November 2007, Local One went on strike for the first time in its history. Most of Broadway went dark for 19 days, including Thanksgiving week.

## What they are fighting about

Here is what is known, in each side's own words.

Local One says it has withdrawn every proposal except wage and benefit increases, to move the talks forward. The employers have reduced their list, but, in the union's words, they "continue to seek dramatic changes to working conditions." The two sides "remain divided over the value of those changes and the wage and benefit increases needed to address them."

The League has not commented publicly. Broadway Journal, which first reported the stalled talks, described the League as "under intensifying pressure from its members to revise work rules and reduce production costs as the industry endures rising failure rates among musicals." A person familiar with the talks told the paper the League is pressing for work-rule changes, while the union considers the financial terms insufficient.

Local 764's dispute adds one more issue. Wardrobe work on Broadway has historically been done mostly by women, at rates lower than stagehand jobs historically held mostly by men. Recent contracts narrowed the gap. According to the union, Local 764 department heads still "must work nearly 45% longer to earn pay comparable to workers in similar department-head roles."

Strip it down and the dispute is simple. The producers want to spend less to put on a show. The crews want to be paid enough to keep up with the cost of living. Both are reasonable. Both cannot fully win.

## Both sides are telling the truth

The easy version picks a villain. The more useful version admits that both sides are under real pressure.

**The producers' pressure is real.** Broadway grossed a record $1.91 billion in the 2025-26 season, according to the League. But a record gross is not a record profit. Musical attendance fell 4.7 percent that season. Production budgets for new musicals now commonly run past $20 million. In December 2025, CNN reported that none of the 18 commercial musicals that opened the prior season had recouped. Producers point to rising theater rent, fees, labor, and materials. Lumber alone, CNN noted, has roughly doubled in price since December 2016.

And producers cannot simply raise prices to cover it. The average Broadway ticket was $131.09 last season, up 1.7 percent. As BroadwayWorld pointed out, that trailed inflation. A family of four can spend more than $500 on musical tickets before dinner. The audience is squeezed too.

**The crews' pressure is real.** A stagehand's week does not end when the curtain comes down. It includes the load-in before a show exists, the maintenance between performances, the 2 a.m. load-out after it closes. Every one of those hours is paid in dollars, and dollars are buying less.

In September 2026, annual U.S. inflation was 3.7 percent. Prices rose 0.6 percent that month and another 0.4 percent in October. Shelter alone drove more than 30 percent of October's increase. Gasoline is up 37.4 percent over the past year. The Federal Reserve is expected to raise interest rates next month. (The Bureau of Labor Statistics could not publish an annual rate for October 2026, because last year's government shutdown left no October 2025 data to compare against.)

Now look at the raises other Broadway unions accepted last year. Actors' Equity's previous contract raised the Broadway minimum 5 percent in the first year and 4 percent in each of the next two. The contract Equity signed in October 2025 raises it 3 percent a year, paired with larger health fund contributions. Equity's own negotiators called the raise "smaller" than the last cycle.

Do the arithmetic on a $2,100 week, close to the 2024 base minimum for a Local One Category A stagehand. A 3 percent raise brings it to $2,163. Keeping pace with 3.7 percent inflation takes $2,178. That is about $15 a week, roughly $760 a year, lost before a single hour of overtime. On paper it is a raise. In the grocery store it is a pay cut.

So each side brings a true statement to the table. The producers say the money is not there. The workers say the money they are paid does not go as far. Both are describing the same thing from opposite ends.

## The same fight, at every table

If this were only a Broadway problem, it would be a Broadway story. It is not.

At Brigham and Women's Hospital in Boston, roughly 4,000 nurses have been bargaining for more than a year. Their contract expired in July. They are pushing for a cost-of-living raise, citing inflation and the cost of living in Boston. The hospital's "best and final" offer contained no across-the-board wage increase. Its parent, Mass General Brigham, says it must bolster its operating margin, and it began layoffs in October.

In California, SEIU Local 1000, about 100,000 state workers, staged a one-day strike on October 21. Its contract expired June 30. The union says the state told it there was "no money" for more compensation.

In Los Angeles, the teachers' union reached a tentative deal only after the school board found more money in a closed session. A UC Berkeley labor expert noted that inflation, then 3.3 percent, was already higher than the teachers' original 3 percent opening ask.

Hospitals, governments, school districts, Broadway producers. Every employer says the same thing: costs are up, margins are thin, there is no money. Every union says the same thing back: our members cannot keep up with prices. And in every case, both statements are true.

That is not a coincidence. It is a pattern. And a pattern has a cause.

## The problem no one puts on the table

Every number in these negotiations is measured in dollars. The wage. The rent. The health fund contribution. The ticket price. The lumber. The producer's budget. The investor's expected return.

When the dollar loses purchasing power year after year, every one of those numbers has to keep rising just to stand still. The theater owner's rent goes up because the building is worth more dollars. The lumber yard charges more because its costs went up. The health insurer charges more. The investor who could earn a higher return on a Treasury bill as interest rates rise wants more for the risk of a musical. The ticket buyer, whose own paycheck is losing ground, can only pay so much.

Then all of it lands at a negotiating table, where a union and a group of producers fight over who has to absorb the loss.

That is the real question being bargained at Proskauer this month. Not whether stagehands are valuable. Nobody seriously argues that. The question is how the loss of purchasing power gets divided: how much the crews give up in real wages, how much the producers give up in margin, how much the audience gives up in ticket prices. It is a negotiation over who gets devalued the least.

As long as everyone at the table measures their life in a unit that is designed to lose value, that negotiation never ends. It comes back every three years, a little harder each time. Every contract becomes a defense against the last three years of inflation, and a guess about the next three.

Until the people on both sides of the table recognize they are negotiating the same problem, they will keep treating each other as the cause of it.

## Without them, there is no show

Bitcoin for the Arts speaks mostly to artists: dancers, actors, musicians, painters. So it is worth saying plainly. Without stagehands, there is no production. Without wardrobe, the quick change does not happen. Without hair and makeup, sound, props, carpenters, electricians, and the flyman on the rail, the curtain does not go up. Artists and crews are one workforce.

Bitcoin for the Arts founder Dion Wilson learned that from both sides of the stage.

As a dance student at SUNY Purchase, Dion spent one semester not performing at all. He worked the technical side of the school's spring concert instead, and learned to operate the lighting board. He sat where the cues get called and watched what it takes to make a dancer's entrance look effortless. It gave him an appreciation of the production side of theater that no amount of time on stage could have.

Years later, as a dancer with the Alvin Ailey American Dance Theater, Dion was out with an injury. During that time, the Ailey stagehands let him work alongside them and help set up one of the productions. He had spent his career knowing what it felt like when the curtain went up at prime time on a Saturday night. He had never known what it was like to take down the lights at the end of that night. He learned it from his IATSE brothers and sisters.

That is the half of every show the audience never sees. Hours before the house opens, and hours after it empties, the crew is there. They work rigorous, around-the-clock schedules so the production happens on time, every time. And now they are in the same position artists have been in for years: fighting to hold on to the value of their work inside a system that quietly takes a little of it every year.

## Why crews may understand Bitcoin faster than anyone

Bitcoin for the Arts teaches artists Bitcoin and sound money principles. It is time to bring that education to every department that makes a production go live: stagehands, wardrobe, hair and makeup, sound, props, scenic artists.

There is a good reason to think the crews will get it fast. Many of the ideas behind Bitcoin are things they already work with every night.

- **A cue list.** A lighting console runs a show from a list of cues, executed in order, the same way every night. Everyone on headset knows where the show is. Bitcoin's ledger is a list of transactions in blocks, in order, that anyone can check. Nobody can quietly rewrite cue 47 after the show has run.
- **The tracking backup.** On a big show, a second console runs in parallel, tracking every move of the first, so that if one fails the show does not stop. Bitcoin works with thousands of copies of the ledger, held independently around the world. There is no single console to unplug.
- **The counterweight.** A fly system works because weight on the arbor balances the load on the pipe. Add scenery, add weight. Bitcoin has its own balance, the difficulty adjustment, which keeps new blocks coming roughly every ten minutes no matter how much computing power joins or leaves the network.
- **The load-out.** You cannot fake a load-out. The trucks are full or they are not. Bitcoin's security works the same way. It is called proof of work, and it rests on real energy spent, which cannot be counterfeited.

And the most important idea is the simplest. There will only ever be 21 million bitcoin. No board, no government, and no producer can print more. A unit of savings that cannot be diluted is the one thing missing from every negotiating table described above.

To be clear about what this is and is not: Bitcoin does not settle this contract. It does not pay this month's rent or replace a pension or a health fund. Its price is volatile, and anyone who holds it has to be ready for that. What it offers is an education in how money works, a savings tool outside the dollar for anyone who chooses it, and a way to understand why every contract feels harder than the last one.

## One workforce, one problem

Artists and crews have been taught to see themselves as separate. Different unions, different contracts, different sides of the curtain. At the table, they even compete for the same slice of a production's budget.

But the dancer whose paycheck buys less every year and the stagehand whose paycheck buys less every year are not competitors. They are two people losing to the same thing. So is the producer watching a musical fail to recoup. So is the audience member deciding whether a family can afford a night at the theater.

Bitcoin for the Arts hopes Local One, Local 764, and the League reach a fair agreement, and soon, before the holidays. Broadway works when everyone who makes it is paid enough to stay. But the next contract will face the same pressure, and the one after that. The way out starts with naming the problem every side shares.
