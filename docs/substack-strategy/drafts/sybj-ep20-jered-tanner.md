## BODY

**Embed the intro clip here.**

The clip is the opening of the episode. A working actor. A festival contract that ends. Couches. A Broadway investment that showed who actually gets paid. And the first time a wallet did what a bank would not.

Jered Tanner and I toured North America together in Disney's The Lion King. He was a standby for Timon, Zazu, and Pumbaa. Three comic tracks, three puppets, three voices. Sometimes all in the same week, and sometimes with no warning. Before the tour, seasons at the Utah Shakespeare Festival. After it, Merlin at Tournament of Kings in Las Vegas. He records as Eon Jred. With his wife Andrea, another Lion King alum, he is half of The Mysteries Gone, and he is the lead singer of Dust Mummies. They have three children.

From the outside, that looks like making it.

I asked him what the money looked like from inside.

Lion King was the first time he made decent money as a theater actor. Before that, a Shakespeare festival might run four months, and then he was unemployed. He did not know to file for unemployment between shows. He floated. He slept on other people's couches. For years. When the good paycheck arrived, he was scared to lose it. On the road he was the one who booked the housing in the next city, so the company would not hand the per diem to a hotel. He did not think the job was forever.

At two years, he was ready to leave. Vegas paid well too. Same role, same show, eight shows a week, sometimes ten. He was making records on the side, and he still could not stay.

"I get really stale and stagnant as an artist when I'm stuck in a spot for two years."

That is the part of a career that never makes the poster. A steady check can be the thing that boxes you in. Corporate theater put him in a role and reminded him he could be replaced. That was never his idea of the job. We got into this work to make something. No paycheck replaces that. We still live in a system where you need the paycheck to get to the next thing you want to make.

We stayed on Broadway, because it is 2026 and a house can be full while the show closes. The costs rise faster than the tickets. Jered lost faith in Broadway a long time ago. He put money into a Broadway production and saw the shape of it: a great deal of money at the front, and very little coming back at the end. Artists, he said, tend not to watch that closely. The people in the room for the business do. Rent, real estate, inflation. A packed house where too many seats were discounted. A famous name who costs more than the run can carry. He has watched shows he admired die that way.

He did not come to Bitcoin through a conference. He came through 2020. GameStop. AMC. A retail crowd that thought it could land one punch on Wall Street, and a brokerage that shut the buy button when the punch landed. He bought Dogecoin near the top and watched it fall the next day. He stepped away for a few years. Then he came back through Bitcoin, and through the larger names his brother and a friend pointed him toward, because he wanted to know where the money was actually going. He built a coin of his own so he could see how that world works, from the inside, the same way he had seen Broadway. He is making a student film about it, called Buy the Dip, while he finishes a graduate degree at Maharishi International University.

The title is a joke he wants to retire. His conclusion is the opposite of the trade. Do not sell. Use it. Accept it as payment.

Along the way he talked with Bernard von NotHaus, who issued the Liberty Dollar and had the gold seized. Jered's point was simple. There was no permitted way to do what Satoshi did. Satoshi did not back Bitcoin with gold. He did not stay to run it. He left.

Then Jered said the sentence a lot of artists are carrying. He was late. If someone had told him in 2014, he would have bought everything he could. He missed it at $1,000. And now the price is already near $100,000.

He had already found the other number himself. Maybe 5 percent of humanity is in this. He is not behind. I asked him to hold the two sentences together, because an artist needs to hear them in the same breath. We are early, and we missed the boat. Both cannot be true.

You did not miss the boat. We are early.

There is a harder version, and I said that one too. I do not think artists are early to the trouble. I wake up to another company in crisis, another contract fight, another show that cannot make the week. We are going to feel it first. We need to understand this before everybody else, because the old system fails on us before it fails on everyone else. We led with our work inside the broken system. We have to be first into the new one.

The full conversation is below. Andrea and the band. What a wallet felt like the first time money moved on a Sunday night. The song Open Source. What he did with the union retirement account: he took it out of the system and put it into equipment and into the work. And the last question we ask every guest. If Bitcoin For The Arts handed him $500 to $2,000 in bitcoin, what would it unlock? Right now, the answer is gear. Lights. Microphones. The tools he had to leave behind.
