# The Jellicle Ball and the Money

*A Broadway musical won three Tony Awards, made history for trans artists, played to a million dollars a week, and closed in five months. Andrew Lloyd Webber says Broadway is in "dire danger." He is right. The problem is not what everyone is saying it is.*

**Bitcoin for the Arts · Substack draft · for publication week of July 14, 2026**

---

## TITLE

Broadway Just Killed Its Most-Awarded New Show in Five Months. The Money Is Why.

## SUBTITLE

Cats: The Jellicle Ball won three Tony Awards, made history for trans artists, grossed a million dollars a week, and still cannot survive the Broadway math. Andrew Lloyd Webber says the American theater is in "dire danger." He is right. The failure is not diversity, taste, or streaming. The failure is that the dollar cannot hold value long enough to fund what Broadway builds. Every artist should understand what this means, because it is the same math that is quietly closing your gallery, your dance company, your symphony, and your local playhouse.

---

## BODY

On Monday, July 13, 2026, the producers of Cats: The Jellicle Ball announced that the show will close on Broadway on August 8, 2026, after five months and change on the boards at the Broadhurst Theatre. On Tuesday, July 14, Andrew Lloyd Webber wrote an eleven-post thread on X arguing that the American commercial theater is on the brink of extinction. Sit with what has to be true for both of those things to be true at the same time.

Cats: The Jellicle Ball is not a flop. It is the most awarded new musical of the 2026 Broadway season, tied by only one other production. It received nine Tony Award nominations. It won three of them, including Best Direction of a Musical for Zhailon Levingston and Bill Rauch, Best Choreography for Omari Wiles and Arturo Lyons, and Best Costume Design for Qween Jean. When Qween Jean walked to that stage, she became the first openly trans woman ever to win a Tony Award. The show reimagined Andrew Lloyd Webber's original as a night inside the Black and Latinx queer ballroom scene of 1980s New York, casting the actual living legends of the ballroom tradition (Junior LaBeija from Paris Is Burning, Leiomy Maldonado, "Tempress" Chasity Moore) alongside Broadway veterans like André De Shields. It received rave reviews. The Guardian gave it four stars and called it "a mighty testament to what is possible when producers look past the traditional scope of Broadway." It was grossing between $900,000 and a million dollars a week in the lead-up to the Tonys.

None of that was enough.

The show cost roughly $18 million to bring to Broadway. At a million dollars a week in top-line receipts, once the theater takes its cut, the union labor takes its cut, the marketing takes its cut, the general management takes its cut, and the royalty pool takes its cut, there is not enough left over each week to pay back an $18 million capitalization on a runway that a large-cast musical can afford. The math simply does not close. So the show closes instead.

**This is the story most people will read.** Great show. Diverse cast. Made history. Ran out of money. Sad.

**This is the story most people will miss.** Broadway had its best year in history in the same season.

The Broadway League reported that the 2025-2026 season grossed a record **$1.91 billion** in ticket sales. That is not a typo. It is the biggest year in the industry's recorded history. In the very same season that Cats: The Jellicle Ball could not survive, three older musicals (The Lion King, Wicked, and Hamilton) produced dependable profit machines. Every other new musical that opened had to fight for the scraps. Tammy Faye closed inside four months. Boop! closed inside four months. Smash closed inside four months. Since the pandemic reopened Broadway, 46 musicals have opened at a total production cost of roughly $800 million. Three of them have recouped their investors' money. Three.

Both things are true. Broadway is booming. Broadway is dying. The only way both can be true is if the growth is entirely concentrated at the top of the stack and the middle of the market is being liquidated.

Andrew Lloyd Webber, the man whose original Cats ran on Broadway for 18 years, wrote this on Tuesday morning:

> "The painful truth is that, with things as they are, bringing almost any new show to Broadway makes little financial sense. The costs are immense. Creators, writers and directors are often forced to accept minimal royalties simply to get work staged. Many now survive on a fixed weekly fee rather than sharing properly in the success of the work they helped to create. How can the next generation build a life in theatre under those conditions? Young creatives cannot live on goodwill alone."

He is right. And the reason he is right is a story almost nobody in the American theater is telling honestly.

## What actually broke

The public conversation about why Cats: The Jellicle Ball is closing sorts itself into three familiar bins.

The first is race and identity. A Broadway musical rooted in Black, Latinx, trans, and queer culture won its Tonys and could not sustain a run. Broadway (the argument goes) will celebrate marginalized artists at award time and stop underwriting their work at box-office time. That observation is not wrong at the surface. It is incomplete.

The second is audience taste. Broadway audiences (the argument goes) skew older, whiter, and more tourist-driven, and are not the audience for ballroom culture at scale. That observation is also not wrong at the surface, and is also incomplete. Rave reviews and a million dollars a week in receipts do not describe an audience that stayed home.

The third is streaming and the general softness of American cultural attention. Netflix, YouTube, TikTok, and video games are (the argument goes) eating the theater's lunch. That observation is not wrong either, and it is still incomplete.

Because all three of those explanations, if they were the whole story, would produce a Broadway that is in decline. Broadway is not in decline. Broadway just had a record year.

What is actually happening is that Broadway is bifurcating. A tiny handful of established mega-hits are absorbing more and more of the top-line dollars, because inflation has pushed ticket prices at those shows into territory ($400, $500, $700 for premium seats) that transfers wealth from the audience to the balance sheet. Everything else, including the most artistically successful new work of the season, is being eaten alive by cost. Rents rise. Union scales rise. Insurance rises. Marketing rises. Fabric rises. Steel rises. Chorus salaries rise. Everything rises. The only thing not rising fast enough to keep up is the audience's willingness to pay more for a ticket to a show they have never heard of.

The Broadway economist Ken Davenport, who publishes a widely read blog on the business, calculates that the industry's recoupment rate (the percentage of shows that pay their investors back) has held stubbornly at about 20% for the last several decades. That is roughly the same rate as it was in the 1960s. What has changed is the scale. A 1987 Andrew Lloyd Webber production could be capitalized at $8 million, in 1987 dollars. A comparable production today costs roughly $30 million. If you inflation-adjust the 1987 figure into 2026 dollars using the official Consumer Price Index, you get about $22 million. So the "official" inflation gets you two-thirds of the way to today's number. The other third is monetary. It is what an honest observer would call debasement.

We are running the same 20% recoupment rate against production costs that have quietly tripled in real terms and quintupled in nominal terms over a single generation. The shows that recoup are the shows that can charge premium ticket prices to a captive tourist audience over a fifteen-year run. The shows that cannot are Cats: The Jellicle Ball. And Tammy Faye. And Boop! And Smash. And every bold new work that tries to open on Broadway in the second half of the 2020s and gets exactly enough runway to fail.

The problem, in one sentence: **the currency Broadway is priced in cannot hold value long enough to fund what Broadway builds.**

## This is not a Broadway problem

Six weeks ago in our newsletter, we ran the story of the Royal Ballet in London cutting 64 roles from its company because the arts council funding that used to cover more than half its operating costs had fallen to 19% of the budget. Two weeks ago we ran New York City's 2027 budget, which set a record $323 million for culture funding and simultaneously opened a new emergency fund to bail out arts organizations because $323 million is not enough. Last quarter we ran the closing of dance companies in Boston as pandemic-era ARPA funding sunset. Every month, the story is the same story. Every month, the fiat number is bigger than the one before, and every month, the ground underneath the artists is shakier than the one before.

Different cities. Different disciplines. Different politicians. Same pattern.

Every headline is a record. Every record is followed by a new emergency fund. Every emergency fund is denominated in a currency that has lost purchasing power every year of Andrew Lloyd Webber's career and every year of the next director trying to break into the industry. The National Endowment for the Arts opened a new funding cycle last week with a July 21 deadline. It is real money to the artist who receives it. It is a thimble against a bathtub.

The chorus dancer in Cats: The Jellicle Ball has to keep training seven days a week, no matter what the dollar does. The set designer has to keep pricing lumber against a materials market that repriced during the pandemic and never came back down. The composer has to keep writing on a runway measured in years, financed against a currency that expects to lose value every one of those years. The scene shop owner in Long Island City has to price a build against a lease that renews annually.

Every one of those humans is being asked to do long-term creative work priced in a unit that cannot hold value across the time horizon their work requires. This is not a Broadway problem. It is the same problem, in the same shape, for the ballet company, the symphony, the museum, the film festival, the small press, the regional theater, the choir, the busker on the subway platform, the gallerist in Chelsea, and the illustrator finishing a picture book at her kitchen table.

If you are an artist and you have not looked squarely at what monetary debasement is doing to your work, you have not yet met the largest structural force acting against you. Not audience taste. Not race. Not streaming. The money.

## What sound money would change

Here is the specific claim, and I want an artist to be able to argue with it or agree with it on the merits. The claim is not that Bitcoin would have saved Cats: The Jellicle Ball this particular season. The claim is deeper.

The claim is that long-form creative work (a Broadway musical, a symphony season, a museum acquisition, a documentary film, a novel, a chorus's annual budget, an artist's studio practice) requires a unit of account that holds value across the time horizon of the work. If it takes ten years to workshop, mount, and recoup a Broadway musical, and the unit the entire economy is priced in loses 2% to 8% of its value every one of those ten years, then the artist has to run twice as fast at every step of the process just to stand still. This is what has been quietly happening to every creative discipline in the developed world for the last several decades. It is why every generation of artists says the previous generation had it easier. They did have it easier. The money was less broken.

Sound money changes that math at the root. Sound money is money whose supply cannot be inflated arbitrarily. It is money whose purchasing power tends to grow over time, not shrink. It is what a savings unit is supposed to be. Bitcoin is a sound money. That is not a religious claim about Bitcoin, it is a description of how its issuance schedule works. Twenty-one million units, absolutely fixed. No emergency fund. No printing press. No mayor with a budget shortfall who can quietly dilute your savings to pay for a new stadium.

When a working artist saves in a unit that does not melt, three things become newly possible.

**One.** The artist can afford to work on projects with longer time horizons. A composer can write for years knowing that the sats she saves this season will still buy tools next season. A theater company can capitalize a production against a treasury that is not being eaten by 8% inflation while the show is in previews. A museum can build an endowment that a fifty-year time horizon does not liquidate.

**Two.** The artist can be paid honestly when the audience pays honestly. Cats: The Jellicle Ball grossed roughly $22 million across its run and could not close its $18 million capitalization gap because too much of that gross was consumed by the running costs of doing business in a currency that debases faster than the show can price it back. If the currency held its value, the same top-line dollars would fund a different kind of theater. Not a magic solution. A less lopsided one.

**Three.** The audience can become the patron again. A million dollars a week in ballroom queens dancing to Andrew Lloyd Webber at the Broadhurst is one of the most beautiful economic sentences of the year. What would it take for that money to reach the actors, the designers, the composers, and the crew directly, in a form they could save? It would take a rail that does not have five middlemen taking a scrape between the ticket buyer and the artist. That rail exists. It is called Lightning, and it is built on Bitcoin. Every artist who accepts a tip in sats has already used it.

## What Bitcoin For The Arts is doing about this

We started Bitcoin For The Arts, Inc. as a US 501(c)(3) nonprofit because it was obvious to us that fixing the money was going to be the fight of the next generation, and that artists were going to be the ones with the most to lose if they did not learn to see it early. Every sponsor we take is offered on a rail that lets the value we raise actually reach the artists on the other end.

We are not going to save Broadway. Broadway is going to have to reckon with what Andrew Lloyd Webber said this week, and the people with actual power in that ecosystem (theater owners, unions, producers) are going to have to reckon with each other. We do not have a Broadway theater to give anyone. We do not have a Tony to hand out.

What we have is a small nonprofit trying to do three things while the money story catches up with everyone else.

**Teach artists the money.** If you are an artist reading this and you have never touched Bitcoin, that is not a moral failing. It is a signal that the culture around you has not yet done its part. That is our job. We publish weekly. We answer email. We host workshops. We have a directory of artists working across every discipline that you can join in three minutes at bitcoinforthearts.org. We do this because we believe the next century of art will belong to the artists who understand the money. Not the ones with the biggest gallery, or the loudest agent, or the largest social following. The artists who understand what a store of value is, why they need one, and how to use it. ## Andrew Lloyd Webber is right

Andrew Lloyd Webber is right about the danger, and he is right that Broadway cannot survive on three old shows. What he did not say, because it is a bridge too far for a man whose entire career has been built inside the current monetary system, is that the danger is not internal to Broadway. The danger is that Broadway is denominated in a unit of account that cannot hold value long enough to fund what Broadway builds. This is not a Broadway failure. It is a system failure that Broadway is currently the most visible casualty of.

Cats: The Jellicle Ball plays its last show at the Broadhurst Theatre on Saturday, August 8, 2026. Zhailon Levingston, Bill Rauch, Qween Jean, Omari Wiles, Arturo Lyons, and every dancer in the company will move on to the next thing. Some of them will change American theater. Some of them will change the world. All of them will be paid, eventually, in dollars that will be worth less than the dollars they were paid in this year.

Until that stops being true, every artist in America is running the same race Cats: The Jellicle Ball just lost.

Bitcoin For The Arts exists so that the artists who understand this early do not have to run it alone.
