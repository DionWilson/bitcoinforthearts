# The Missing Word

*A theater veteran named Brian D. Frey wrote the sharpest Broadway postmortem of the year on Cats: The Jellicle Ball. It is rigorous, precise, and completely correct. It is also missing the one word that would have made everything he described inevitable. This is not a rebuttal. This is a completion. And it is a letter to every working artist who has ever wondered why the industry they love keeps failing them in exactly the same way, year after year.*

## BODY

A Facebook post appeared in the timeline of Bitcoin for the Arts founder Dion Wilson last week. It came from a man named Brian D. Frey, a Tampa-based theater veteran who spent 32 years in the business as a performer, lighting designer, production manager, upper manager, and board member. He does not currently work in theater full-time, and he says so. He also says, correctly, that walking away from the payroll is what makes him able to write what he wrote without it costing him a job.

His post is the sharpest short piece of Broadway economic analysis to circulate this year. It picks apart the closure of Cats: The Jellicle Ball, the reinvention of Andrew Lloyd Webber's original as a night inside the Black and Latinx queer ballroom scene of 1980s New York, which closed on Saturday, August 9, 2026, after five months on the Broadhurst stage. Three Tony Awards. 94% average capacity. 159,000 people through the door. Two consecutive house records in the final weeks after the closing notice went up. And still, it never had a chance.

Frey does the math on camera, for the record, in public, so nobody can look away.

The Broadhurst has 1,218 seats. Eight shows a week. That is 9,744 seats to sell. Playbill has them averaging $111.29 a ticket. Perfect week, every seat filled, standing room and dust, gets you about $1.08 million in gross. The reported weekly running cost was just under a million dollars. At full capacity, in a theater built to fit into the corner of a Manhattan block, the show cleared perhaps eighty thousand dollars a week against an eighteen million dollar capitalization. Three and a half years of unbroken hundred-percent houses, with no heat waves, no cast illnesses, no slow Januaries, no unforeseen anything, to get investors back to zero.

That was the ceiling. The plan required beating the ceiling.

Frey names, correctly, the moment the show failed. He locates it in a room in 2024, when the size of the house, the size of the company, and the size of the capitalization were negotiated into place. By opening night, he writes, it was already over. Everything after that, the reviews, the awards, the discourse, the sold-out final week, was watching it happen.

He extends the argument beyond Broadway. He names the American nonprofit theater movement, which he says is dying at a rate of two to three companies per month per NEA counts, though nobody is keeping an official tally. He names Southern Rep, Book-It Repertory, San Diego Rep, Triad Stage, Long Wharf, the Mark Taper. He names Oskar Eustis, who three weeks ago announced his departure from The Public Theater and told the Los Angeles Times that the American nonprofit theater movement is over. Frey adds: "I don't know that I disagree with him."

He names the audience number that keeps executive directors up at night. Between 25 and 30 percent of the pre-pandemic audience never came back after March 2020. Not because they were afraid. Because live theater simply stopped being the thing they do, and something else filled the slot. That audience is not coming back on its own, and the field spent the last six years budgeting like it would.

His prescription is right and specific. Right-size now while it is still your call to make. Get months of cash on hand in front of your board. Emergency appeals work exactly once, and after that, donors can tell the difference between a hard year and a broken model. Do not let a closing notice be the thing that finally gets someone in the seat.

Every word of it is correct.

## The word that never appears

Here is what stopped Bitcoin for the Arts on the first read.

In a piece that runs approximately two thousand words about the economics of live theater, the word *inflation* never appears. Neither does *monetary policy*. Neither does *purchasing power*. Neither does *central bank*. Neither does *fiat*. Neither does *dollar debasement*. Neither does *M2*.

Frey writes about money the entire way through. Every figure he cites is a dollar figure. Every diagnosis he offers is delivered in dollars. But the causal frame that would connect every symptom he describes to a single root cause is not present in the piece.

This is not his oversight. This is what our entire culture does. Newspapers write about "wage stagnation" without writing about the monetary system that is stagnating wages. Trade publications write about "rising production costs" without writing about the system that is raising them. Nonprofit boards discuss "audience contraction" without ever asking why 25 to 30 percent of the middle class evaporated from discretionary spending categories in the same six-year window that the M2 money supply expanded by roughly forty percent.

Artists are especially not taught to make these connections. In no undergraduate or graduate performing arts program in the United States is there a required class on monetary theory. Actors, dancers, directors, and designers spend a decade honing their craft, another decade building a career, and no meaningful portion of that education ever explains how the currency they are paid in loses value over time, or why the rent on the studio doubles between contracts, or why health insurance premiums outpace their raises every single year for their entire working lives.

This is what Bitcoin for the Arts exists to correct. Not to make artists into Bitcoiners. To make artists into people who can see what is happening to them.

## What inflation actually is

Because this word is going to do a great deal of work in what follows, it is worth naming precisely what it means, especially for a reader who was never handed a working definition in school.

In everyday conversation, inflation is treated as a synonym for "prices going up." That is the visible symptom. It is not the underlying event. Inflation, in its rigorous economic sense, is an expansion of the money supply. When more dollars enter the economy without a corresponding increase in the goods and services those dollars can buy, each individual dollar becomes less valuable. Prices rise because dollars are worth less, not because bread or theater tickets have become intrinsically more precious. This distinction matters, because it locates the cause of inflation not in butchers or bakers or ticket-sellers, but in the institution that controls how many dollars exist. In the United States, that institution is the Federal Reserve.

The scoreboard the Federal Reserve publishes to measure how many dollars are in the American economy is called *M2*. When this article refers to M2, it is referring to that specific measure. M2 counts physical cash, checking-account balances, savings-account balances, and money market funds. It is a public number, released monthly, and anyone can look it up. When the M2 number goes up, more dollars have entered the system. When it goes up faster than the actual economy grows, each dollar in your paycheck buys less than it did before. That is inflation at its source.

## Why the nut got to a million dollars a week

Let us complete Frey's analysis with the piece he does not have.

He is correct that the running cost of Cats: The Jellicle Ball at the Broadhurst was just under a million dollars a week. That number is not a mystery. It is the sum of union labor, house crew, real estate, insurance, marketing, materials, energy, front-of-house staffing, music licensing, royalties, general management, and the general operating overhead that a Broadway show carries. Every one of those lines is priced in dollars.

Every one of those lines has been inflating faster than the ability of the ticket-buying public to keep up.

The M2 money supply of the United States expanded from roughly $15.4 trillion in February 2020 to approximately $23.2 trillion by mid-2026. That is a fifty-percent expansion of the base money in circulation in the span of six and a half years. Every dollar in the system now competes for goods and services against fifty percent more dollars than it did before the pandemic. The purchasing power of a 2020 dollar today is somewhere between sixty and seventy cents, depending on which basket of goods you use to measure it.

Union contracts renew at rates that track this. Rent renews at rates that track this. Health insurance premiums renew at rates that track this. Materials cost more. Insurance costs more. Energy costs more. Marketing to a distracted, algorithm-fed audience costs more. Every single input into the production of a Broadway show costs more, and every year that goes by, it costs still more, and there is no world in which those costs stop climbing so long as the currency they are denominated in continues to be diluted at scale.

But ticket revenue is not indexed to inflation. It is bounded by the disposable income of the American consumer, which has not kept pace. Real wages, measured against actual cost of living, have been flat or slightly negative for the American middle class since roughly 2020. The people who used to buy Broadway tickets three or four times a year are now buying one, or none, because the seventy dollars they had to spend in 2020 became somewhere between fifty and sixty dollars in real purchasing power by 2026, and the ticket in front of them went from $111 to $140 in the same window.

The scissors closed.

That is why the running cost went to a million dollars a week. Not because the union got greedy. Not because the real estate agent got greedy. Not because the marketing agency got greedy. Because every one of them is charging in dollars, and the dollars they are charging in are worth less every year, so the number of dollars they need to name goes up every year, mechanically, without anyone deciding it should. This is what inflation is. It is not a discrete event. It is the arithmetic of monetary expansion, applied silently, every day, to every price in the economy.

The show did not fail because the title was tired. It did not fail because the audience did not love the cast. It did not fail because the reviews were mixed. It failed because the number on the expense side and the number on the revenue side were pulled apart by monetary forces neither the producers nor the audience had any conscious contact with, and the gap between them was insurmountable before opening night.

## Why 25 to 30 percent of the audience never came back

Frey names the number, honestly, and says the field spent the last six years budgeting as if that audience was coming back. He is right that it is not coming back. He is not asked, and does not answer, why.

The received wisdom, the one you see in industry publications and hear from boards, is that COVID changed viewing habits. That Netflix, Disney+, and TikTok took the leisure hours that used to belong to Broadway, regional theater, dance, and orchestras. That the pandemic was a lifestyle inflection point and habits are hard to reset.

This is true and it is also incomplete. The full truth is that between 2020 and 2026, the American middle class lost roughly twenty to thirty percent of its real purchasing power, and the first line item that gets cut when purchasing power contracts is discretionary entertainment. Live theater is the most discretionary of discretionary purchases. The seat costs more, the parking costs more, the babysitter costs more, the dinner beforehand costs more, and the family that used to have two hundred dollars of disposable income for a Saturday night out now has one hundred and thirty dollars, and the entire evening on Broadway now costs three hundred.

The audience did not fall in love with something else. The audience got poorer. Theater tickets got more expensive. The math of "should we go tonight" changed for tens of millions of American households, and the mathematics of that change is not measured in preferences. It is measured in dollars that used to buy tickets and no longer do.

This is why the sudden burst of demand in the final weeks of Jellicle Ball is so revealing. The demand was there the entire time. The audience wanted to see it. What they did not have was the disposable income to prioritize it against every other thing that was also inflating out of their reach, until the deadline of the closing notice re-ranked their spending decisions and they scraped together the $140 to be part of the last week.

If tickets had been priced at 60% of what they were in 2020 dollars, the show would have sold out every night, all five months, at a Broadhurst that was already averaging 94% capacity. The audience was there. The dollars were not.

## What Frey's prescription misses

Frey tells nonprofit theater owners, boards, and executive directors to right-size now. He tells them to know their months of cash on hand. He tells them that emergency appeals work exactly once. All of it is correct and all of it will help, this season.

None of it addresses the fact that next year, and the year after, the number will inflate again.

If a nonprofit theater right-sizes its budget in 2026 to match the dollars it can actually raise, and the currency continues to be diluted at the current pace, that same theater will need to right-size again in 2028, and again in 2030, and every renewal cycle it will be smaller, thinner, and more precarious than the one before. The trajectory does not stop. Right-sizing is not a fix. Right-sizing is a slower fail.

The only true fix, the only intervention that changes the direction of the arrow, is to change the money the sector operates in. Not to change what dollars buy, which is impossible because dollars are governed by decisions no arts organization has any say in. To change what dollars are, for the sector.

## What sound money is

The term *sound money* is not commonly used in the American conversation about the economy, but it is neither new nor esoteric. It has a specific meaning that comes out of two centuries of monetary theory and human experience. A currency is sound if its total supply cannot be expanded at will by any individual, institution, corporation, or state. That is the entire definition.

Gold, historically, was sound money because gold could not be manufactured by decree. New gold entered the supply only through the physical labor of mining, which was slow and expensive. When human societies were on a gold standard, the total quantity of money in circulation grew at approximately the rate of the productive economy, and prices remained roughly stable across generations. A working man in 1900 who saved a hundred dollars could reasonably expect to buy a similar quantity of goods with those hundred dollars in 1920. The saver was rewarded by patience, and the borrower was penalized by profligacy. This is how money is supposed to work in a healthy economy.

When the United States left the gold standard in 1971, that constraint was removed. The dollar became a purely political instrument, whose supply expands and contracts according to the decisions of a central bank whose mandate is not price stability but full employment, financial system solvency, and, in extremis, the funding of federal deficits. The result is what every American under fifty has lived through. The dollar has lost approximately 85% of its 1971 purchasing power. That loss did not fall equally. It fell hardest on savers, on wage-earners in industries with slow-moving pay structures, on artists, on retirees on fixed incomes, and on any citizen without access to appreciating assets.

The American theater is one of the industries with a slow-moving pay structure. It is also an industry whose customers are almost entirely wage-earners with slow-moving pay structures. It could not be more perfectly designed to be crushed between the millstones of an expanding money supply.

Sound money reverses this dynamic. If a theater company plans a season in a monetary unit whose supply cannot be expanded, and if its labor contracts, its rent, and its ticket prices are all denominated in the same unit, then the production costs of that season are locked at the moment of budgeting. There is no annual erosion. There is no inflating nut. There is no scissors closing between costs and revenue year after year. The season either works or does not work on the day the budget is drawn up, and if it works, it continues to work, indefinitely.

## Bitcoin, briefly, without evangelism

Bitcoin is the first credibly sound money that has existed at scale in the modern era. Its total supply is capped at twenty-one million units. That cap is enforced by a protocol that no individual, corporation, or state has the authority to change. Its issuance schedule is fixed and public. It does not require trust in any human institution. It has been in continuous operation for over seventeen years, without interruption, held by more than 200 million people, and settled at a scale that dwarfs many national payment networks.

None of that requires anyone to believe Bitcoin is the future. It only requires the observation that Bitcoin, as it exists today, is the only monetary unit currently available whose supply is genuinely fixed and whose purchasing power has appreciated, rather than eroded, across the working lifetime of every American artist under the age of sixty.

Now do the exercise Frey never did. Convert his figures. The eighteen-million-dollar capitalization of Cats: The Jellicle Ball, at 2024 Bitcoin prices, was roughly 283 BTC. If the show had been capitalized in Bitcoin, and if its weekly running cost had been denominated in sats rather than dollars, and if its ticket prices had been in sats, the entire arithmetic of the production would be different in a specific and quantifiable way. The 283 BTC of capitalization, held by investors, would have appreciated against every fiat currency over the same period. The weekly nut, denominated in sats, would not have inflated. The ticket prices, denominated in sats, would have remained purchasable by an audience whose sats savings would also have appreciated. The demand that finally showed up in the last two weeks of the run, the demand that broke the house record twice consecutively, would have been present the entire run, because the audience would not have been priced out of the theater by fiat inflation in the interim.

The show that closed after five months at 94% capacity could plausibly have run four to five years and returned every investor with a profit.

None of this is a hypothetical. Every number in it is calculable from the historical price of Bitcoin over the relevant period. Any producer who does the math will find the same answer.

## The gift being offered here

Bitcoin for the Arts is founded and led by a working artist who spent twenty-five years in the industry. Alvin Ailey American Dance Theater. Dance Theatre of Harlem. Philadanco. Disney's *The Lion King* on Broadway. The founder danced on the same stages, in the same houses, under the same fiat monetary system, watching the same slow contraction of budgets, salaries, and health benefits that Frey names in his post, and he did not understand any of it while it was happening.

The gift being offered here is not Bitcoin. The gift being offered here is *understanding*. It is the moment when a working artist reads a piece like Frey's and recognizes, for the first time, that there is a chapter of the story they were never taught to see. That the number on their pay stub, the rent on the studio, the shrinking budgets of the companies they love, the vanishing of the middle-class audience, the growing precarity of every regional theater in America, are not disconnected phenomena. They are the same phenomenon. And that phenomenon has a name.

Once you know that name, you cannot unknow it. You can still choose to keep working inside the fiat system, and most artists will, because that is where the jobs are and the rents are and the audience is. You can still choose to trust that boards and executive directors and philanthropic foundations will figure it out on your behalf, and sometimes they will. But you will make those choices with your eyes open. You will know why the nut is a million dollars a week and why the audience shrunk and why the model quit working. You will not have to accept the mystery that every industry publication offers you, the story that says "audiences changed" or "streaming won" or "the culture moved on." You will know what actually moved.

## The last word

Brian D. Frey ended his post with a line worth repeating: "The expense line doesn't read the internet."

He is right. The expense line does not read the internet, the reviews, the discourse, or the opinions of anybody who has ever had a strong feeling about a Broadway show. What the expense line does read, whether the industry names it or not, is monetary policy. Every year, whether anyone in the room understands it or not, the expense line reads the currency it is denominated in. And every year, the currency it is denominated in becomes less sound, less predictable, less capable of holding its ground against the labor of the people it purports to compensate.

That is the story. It has been the story the entire time. Cats: The Jellicle Ball did not die at the Broadhurst on August 9. It died the moment the entire American economy was pinned to a currency whose supply is expanded at will, and the theater industry, along with every other industry that depends on the discretionary spending of the middle class, was locked into an escalator moving in the wrong direction.

Bitcoin for the Arts exists so that the next generation of working artists does not have to spend their careers riding that escalator without understanding what it is doing to them. Whether any individual artist ever holds a single satoshi is not the point. The point is that the word "inflation" belongs in every serious postmortem of the theater business from this point forward, alongside the words "budget," "capitalization," "labor," "rent," and "audience." Without that word, the entire analysis is a description of symptoms with the diagnosis missing.

Brian D. Frey wrote the sharpest short piece of Broadway analysis of the year. It is remarkable, and everyone in the industry should read it. What we are offering here is one more word, added to the vocabulary, so that when the next production fails, and the next theater closes, and the next audience shrinks, the people watching it happen will finally have the language to name what is doing it.
