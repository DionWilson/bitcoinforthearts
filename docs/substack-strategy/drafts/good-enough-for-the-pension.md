# Good Enough for the Pension

*New York's public pension fund holds $89 million in companies whose value moves with Bitcoin. New York's attorney general told Congress digital assets are "uniquely unsuitable for retirement savings." A New York artist on Cash App cannot receive a Lightning payment. These are not the decisions of "the state." They are the decisions of named people, and this is their record.*

## TITLE

Good Enough for the Pension

## SUBTITLE

New York's pension fund added to its Strategy stake this fall. The same state limits how its residents can use Bitcoin as money. Here are the people who decide both, in their own words and filings.

## BODY

On November 17, 2026, the Office of the New York State Comptroller filed a Form 13F with the Securities and Exchange Commission for the New York State Common Retirement Fund. A 13F is a quarterly list of the stocks a large investor holds. This one covers September 30, 2026.

On it, under "STRATEGY INC-CL A," is a line that reads 352,850 shares, valued at $54,017,807. At the end of June the same fund reported 319,850 shares. It added 33,000 shares in the quarter, an increase of about 10 percent.

Strategy is the company, formerly MicroStrategy, whose main business is buying and holding Bitcoin. It owns hundreds of thousands of them. When Bitcoin rises, the stock tends to rise faster. When Bitcoin falls, the stock tends to fall harder.

Strategy is not the only line. The same filing lists Coinbase, the largest U.S. Bitcoin exchange, and five Bitcoin mining companies:

| Company | Shares | Value at Sept 30, 2026 |
|---|---:|---:|
| Strategy (MSTR) | 352,850 | $54,017,807 |
| Coinbase (COIN) | 109,433 | $20,399,406 |
| IREN (listed as Iris Energy) | 154,600 | $6,320,048 |
| Hut 8 | 36,400 | $3,134,040 |
| Riot Platforms | 125,540 | $2,529,631 |
| MARA Holdings | 130,219 | $1,475,381 |
| CleanSpark | 88,937 | $1,145,509 |
| **Total** | | **$89,021,822** |

Every one of those positions is marked "SOLE" in the filing's investment discretion column. That means the fund, not an outside client, controls the decision to hold them.

That is the money set aside to pay the pensions of New York's state and local public employees: teachers' aides, corrections officers, clerks, sanitation workers, retirees. Bitcoin is part of that portfolio now. Nobody held a press conference about it.

### This is not about "the state"

When a policy is unpopular, it gets described as something the state did, or the city did, or the government did. That language protects the people who made the call. A state does not file a 13F. A state does not sign a bill. A state does not write a letter to Congress.

People do. So here are the people, what each one controls, and what each one has done.

| Person | Office | What they control |
|---|---|---|
| Thomas P. DiNapoli | New York State Comptroller | Sole trustee of the Common Retirement Fund, an estimated $309.7 billion at June 30, 2026 |
| Kathy Hochul | Governor of New York | Appoints the superintendent of the Department of Financial Services, the agency that runs the BitLicense. Signs or vetoes state law. |
| Kaitlin Asrow | Acting Superintendent, Department of Financial Services (DFS) | Runs the BitLicense and decides which companies may offer Bitcoin services to New Yorkers |
| Letitia James | New York Attorney General | The state's chief law enforcement officer. Brings cases against crypto firms and lobbies Congress on crypto law |
| Zohran Mamdani | Mayor of New York City | No authority over the BitLicense. His record on Bitcoin comes from his years in the state Assembly. |

The three statewide elected officials above, Hochul, DiNapoli, and James, all won reelection on November 3, 2026, by wide margins. These decisions will continue. That is why they are worth understanding.

## The comptroller and the pension

Comptroller DiNapoli's own office describes his role this way: "As the sole trustee responsible for the New York State Common Retirement Fund (Fund), Comptroller DiNapoli is directly accountable for its performance, oversight and management."

Most large pensions are run by boards. New York's is run by one person. That is not a criticism. It is the structure, and it means there is one name attached to what the fund owns.

Fairness matters here, because the honest version of this story is stronger than the angry one.

The fund's written investment policy says its internally managed stocks use "passive management strategies" meant to "approximate closely the returns and exposure of the broad market indices." In plain language, the fund buys the whole market in proportion, the way an index fund does. It does not usually pick individual stocks.

So the most likely reason the fund owns more Strategy is not that someone in Albany fell in love with Bitcoin. Strategy has grown into a large company in the indexes a fund like this tracks, it keeps issuing new shares to buy more Bitcoin, and an index fund has to keep buying to keep up. The 13F does not give a reason, and the comptroller's office has not given one publicly. But that is how indexing works.

That is the point. The market has already priced Bitcoin into the companies that make up a normal retirement portfolio. A public pension that simply owns "the market" now owns Bitcoin exposure by default. To avoid it, the trustee would have to carve it out on purpose. He has not.

Two more facts belong here, because the people reading this deserve the whole picture.

First, the position is small. Strategy was about 0.08 percent of the stocks reported on the 13F, and a far smaller share of the full $309.7 billion fund. Nobody's pension is riding on it.

Second, it is volatile. Strategy closed September at about $153 a share. On November 17, the day of the filing, it closed at $96.50, about 37 percent lower. At that price the same 352,850 shares were worth roughly $34 million. The fund has held the stock through that kind of swing before. It held 7,459 shares at the end of 2022. After Strategy's 10-for-1 stock split in August 2024, it held 86,850. It now holds 352,850.

That is what a long-term holder looks like. The fund does not panic out of a position because the price fell. It keeps the exposure and lets time do the work.

Remember that, because the next official says the opposite.

## The attorney general and "retirement savings"

In April 2025, Attorney General Letitia James wrote to the leaders of the congressional committees writing federal crypto law. Among her requests was "disallowing digital assets in retirement accounts." Her letter explained why:

> "Digital assets are uniquely unsuitable for retirement savings due to their high volatility. The underlying value of cryptocurrency is unpredictable and not determined by true price discovery because they have no intrinsic value on which their prices are based."

She went further, warning that the Bitcoin held to back crypto ETFs, "a popular choice for retirement funds," is "at risk of permanent theft." In July 2025 she repeated the warning in a statement to the U.S. Senate Banking Committee: "Given the extremely risky and volatile nature of digital assets, mom and pop retail investors" need more protection, not less.

Precision matters here too. The state pension does not hold Bitcoin itself, or a spot Bitcoin ETF. The 13F lists none. It holds stocks of companies whose value is tied to Bitcoin. And Attorney General James's request was about "retirement accounts." The state pension is a different kind of plan, a defined-benefit pension.

But step back and look at what the two offices are saying together.

The state's chief lawyer tells Congress that Bitcoin is "uniquely unsuitable for retirement savings." In the same years, the state's own retirement fund is building an $89 million position in Bitcoin's largest corporate holder, its largest U.S. exchange, and its miners. And it is holding through a 37 percent drawdown with the patience of someone who expects the asset to be worth more later.

Both cannot be the considered view of New York's government. One office is treating Bitcoin as too dangerous for you to hold in your retirement account. The other is holding Bitcoin exposure for the people who work for the state.

## The regulator and the menu

The Department of Financial Services runs the BitLicense. It was designed under Benjamin Lawsky, New York's first superintendent of financial services, and took effect on August 8, 2015. Any company that holds, transmits, buys, sells, or exchanges Bitcoin for New Yorkers needs one, or a limited purpose trust charter from the same department.

When it took effect, companies left. Kraken, one of the oldest Bitcoin exchanges, cut off New York. Its founders wrote: "Regrettably, the abominable BitLicense has awakened." Bitfinex, Poloniex, LocalBitcoins, Paxful, and others left too. The New York Business Journal called it "the Great Bitcoin Exodus." The application fee was $5,000, but companies told Fortune the real cost of applying ran from about $50,000 to $100,000 in legal work, which shut out the small startups.

Eleven years later, Kraken still does not serve New York. Its licensing page lists two states it does not serve: New York and Maine.

Today the department is run by Kaitlin Asrow, whom Governor Hochul appointed acting superintendent effective October 18, 2025. Before that, Asrow led the department's virtual currency licensing and supervision. In February 2026 she told the state legislature what she was proud of:

> "Under my leadership, we tripled the headcount of our virtual currency regulatory team, revamped and strengthened supervision, issued 11 pieces of nation-leading guidance, and collected more than $200 million in enforcement actions. Today, New York's virtual currency regime is regarded as the strongest in the world."

Read that list again. Headcount. Guidance. Enforcement dollars. Nothing on it counts how many New Yorkers can actually use Bitcoin as money. That is not a gotcha. It is a description of what the job rewards. A regulator is measured on what goes wrong, not on what never gets built.

Here is what that looks like from a phone in Brooklyn, according to the companies' own pages:

- **River**, a Bitcoin-only brokerage, serves "every US state except Nevada and New York."
- **Cash App**, which is licensed in New York, says in its own disclosures: "Lightning Network, Square Bitcoin, and Bitkey are not available to New York residents." Lightning is the network that moves small Bitcoin payments instantly for fractions of a cent. Square Bitcoin is how a Square merchant takes Bitcoin at the counter. Bitkey is Block's self-custody wallet.
- **Coinbase** does not offer its Bitcoin-backed loans to New York residents.

Credit where it is due. The menu has improved. Strike, a Bitcoin-focused company, received its BitLicense in February 2026 and opened to New Yorkers in March. Coinbase, Gemini, Robinhood, PayPal, and Cash App all let New Yorkers buy Bitcoin. And any New Yorker with a brokerage account can buy Strategy stock or a spot Bitcoin ETF, exactly as the pension does. The BitLicense does not cover stocks.

So the accurate line is not "the pension gets Bitcoin and you don't." It is this:

**You can buy the paper. What New York makes harder is using Bitcoin as money.**

That distinction is the whole thing. The pension holds stocks. A stock is a claim on a company that holds Bitcoin. You cannot pay a fellow artist with a share of Strategy. You cannot take Strategy at your merch table. The part of Bitcoin that matters most to a working artist is the part with the most friction in New York.

## Why this is an artist's issue

Bitcoin for the Arts sells its podcast episodes on Fanfares for 21 sats each. A sat is one hundred-millionth of a Bitcoin. The payment travels over Lightning and lands in seconds. Part of it goes straight to the artist on the episode.

That is the circular economy this organization exists to build. An artist prices work in sats, earns in sats, and pays other artists in sats. No platform takes 30 percent. No bank fee eats a small payment from overseas.

A New York artist who keeps their Bitcoin in Cash App cannot receive that Lightning payment in Cash App. A New York painter with a Square reader at a weekend market cannot switch on Square Bitcoin. The tools are built, licensed in New York, and switched off for New Yorkers by the company, under rules set in Albany.

There is good news, and it is the most useful paragraph in this article. **You do not need a license to hold your own Bitcoin or to accept it for your own work.** The BitLicense applies to businesses that hold or move Bitcoin for other people. A New York artist who uses a self-custody Lightning wallet, one where you hold your own keys, can receive sats for a print tonight. The restriction falls on the companies that would do it for you, which means it falls hardest on the people least comfortable doing it themselves.

## The governor, the moratorium, and the mayor

Governor Hochul's link to all of this runs through two powers: she appoints the person who runs DFS, and she signs or vetoes state law.

On November 22, 2022, she signed Chapter 628 of the Laws of 2022. It placed a two-year moratorium on new and renewed air permits for fossil-fuel power plants supplying electricity directly to proof-of-work mining, the energy-based security system Bitcoin uses. It was the first law of its kind in the country. The legislature had passed it in June. Politico reported that Hochul "punted on the issue for months" and signed it two weeks after she was elected to a full term.

The moratorium was narrow. It did not ban owning, trading, or mining Bitcoin, and it expired on November 22, 2024. But it put New York on record as the first state to single out Bitcoin's energy use for a legal pause. Four years later, that state's pension fund owns five Bitcoin mining companies.

One of the Assembly members listed as a cosponsor of that moratorium bill, A7389C, was Zohran Mamdani, now mayor of New York City.

The mayor has no power over the BitLicense. Financial regulation comes from Albany and Washington. It would be unfair to blame him for DFS rules, and this article does not. But his record on Bitcoin is public, and it is consistent. In 2023, as an Assembly member, he supported Attorney General James's crypto consumer protection bill, saying, "When crypto companies collapse, it isn't the rich who suffer, it's small investors who disproportionately come from low-income and communities of color." In January 2026, he said he holds no crypto and would not buy the memecoin his predecessor had promoted.

That concern for small investors is real, and the losses are real. The FBI counted 318 complaints of cryptocurrency kiosk fraud in New York State, totaling $5,777,640 in losses, figures cited in a 2026 New York City Council resolution. Scams exist. Collapses like FTX happened. Bitcoin for the Arts has no interest in pretending otherwise.

But protecting small people and keeping small people out are different things. Fraud at a crypto kiosk is not a reason to block a working artist from receiving a 21-sat Lightning payment. A rule that leaves the well-advised with full access, through brokers and a $309.7 billion pension, while narrowing the tools ordinary people use, lands on the wrong people.

## The comptroller already found the cracks

One more record belongs in this article, and it comes from the comptroller himself.

In January 2024, Comptroller DiNapoli's auditors reviewed how DFS runs the BitLicense. They found "limited assurance that DFS is adequately performing its oversight responsibilities related to the application for and supervision of virtual currency licenses in the state, creating the risk that licenses could be granted to" applicants who should not get them. At the time, DFS supervised 21 virtual currency licensees holding more than $175 billion in assets.

In January 2026, the comptroller's follow-up audit found that of six recommendations, three had been fully implemented and three only partially.

So by the state's own auditor, the BitLicense has been a high wall with an uneven gate. It is hard enough to keep Kraken and River out for a decade. It was not, by the comptroller's findings, consistently rigorous with the companies it let in. A regime with that record does not get to call itself protection without being asked to show its work.

## How the system thinks

Strip away the names and the pattern is simple. Each office is responding to what its job rewards.

**The pension trustee** is rewarded for tracking the market at low cost. The market includes Bitcoin now, so the pension holds Bitcoin exposure, quietly and patiently.

**The attorney general** is rewarded for preventing and punishing harm. So she warns Congress about volatility and theft and asks to keep Bitcoin out of retirement accounts.

**The regulator** is rewarded for supervision, guidance, and enforcement dollars. Nobody measures her by how many New Yorkers can use Lightning, so that number does not move.

**The governor** is rewarded for managing competing interests. She held the mining bill until after an election, then signed it.

None of this requires a villain. It does require naming who decides. When a fund under one trustee holds Bitcoin exposure, and an agency under one appointee narrows how residents use the asset, and the chief lawyer calls it unsuitable for retirement, "the state" is not a single mind. It is a set of people, each protected by the word "state," each making a choice that someone else pays for.

Artists are usually the ones who pay. We were taught that money is a subject for other people, so we accept the menu we are handed.

## What New Yorkers can do with this

This is education, not financial advice. Do your own research and make the choices that fit your life.

1. **Read the filing yourself.** The 13F is public at sec.gov. Search for "New York State Common Retirement Fund." The Strategy line is real, and you do not need to trust anyone's summary of it, including ours.
2. **Check your app, not just your Bitcoin.** Look up whether Lightning, merchant payments, and withdrawals to your own wallet work for New York residents on the service you use. The answer is often different from the rest of the country.
3. **Learn self-custody.** Holding your own keys is the one Bitcoin tool no license can switch off. It is also the one that needs the most care. Bitcoin for the Arts teaches it, free.
4. **Take a payment in sats.** Price one print, one lesson, or one track in sats and receive it to a wallet you control. That is the circular economy, one transaction at a time.
5. **Ask the offices directly, and ask on the record.** Ask the comptroller's office whether Bitcoin exposure in the pension is an index decision or a deliberate one. Ask DFS why a licensed company still cannot offer Lightning to New Yorkers. Ask the attorney general how she squares "uniquely unsuitable for retirement savings" with the state's own retirement fund. When DFS proposes a rule, it opens a public comment period. Use it.

New Yorkers do not need a different set of rules than the pension fund. They need the same access to the asset the pension fund is already holding.

Stay curious, stay steady, and keep creating.
