The clip is the opening of the episode. A nurse practitioner's son in the back woods of New Hampshire. A food company whose own people would not feed the product to their kids. An album that earned pennies for a decade. And the sentence underneath all of it: the rails for paying an artist directly are already built.

There will only ever be 21 million bitcoin.

That number is the whole design. No board can vote to issue a 22nd million. No producer, no label, and no government can dilute the supply to make the next budget easier. Every bitcoin that will ever exist is already spoken for by that limit. The work now is deciding who it serves.

Bitcoin For The Arts just finished a smaller version of the same count.

Abel James is the 21st artist to sit down for Share Your Bitcoin Journey. Twenty-one working artists. One season. One conversation each about the money behind the work, told by the person who does the work. Dancers, musicians, actors, painters, builders. People who were never taught this in school, saying out loud what their industry actually pays.

Twenty-one is not a round number we picked for a poster. It is the number the money itself stops at. One artist in this season for every million bitcoin that will ever exist. A small count, on purpose. Small enough to finish. Small enough to mean each person was actually heard.

The season is the proof of concept. The next one is the work.

## He questioned the expert, then he questioned the money

Abel James grew up in New Hampshire. His mother was a nurse practitioner. Antibiotics made her sons sick, so she learned the plants and made the medicines herself. Abel left that world. He finished high school and college in six years, studied brain science and music at Dartmouth, and took a consulting job to pay off the loans. The job came with what they called the Cadillac health plan. He used it. He found a well-rated doctor and followed the instructions exactly. Less meat. No eggs. More grains.

Eighteen months later he had gained about 30 pounds. His labs had gone the wrong direction. The problems the diet was supposed to prevent had arrived on schedule.

He did not figure it out in a clinic. He came home in Austin and found his building on fire. He lost his clothes, his things, the ordinary shape of a life. In someone else's shirt, he looked in the mirror and did not recognize the person the advice had built. So he went back to real food, and he stopped eating all day out of fear. In a few months the weight was gone and he was running personal bests. He was angry that getting well had required him to ignore the expert. He started writing about it. Then he started a podcast. He has been interviewing people who got well and stayed well for more than 15 years. The show is The Fat-Burning Man. He is also a New York Times bestselling author.

The habit transferred.

Years later he was looking at a starter house in Austin. One bathroom. Nothing fancy. He bought it for a little under $300,000. A few years earlier the same house had been $80,000. The year his work finally paid, after an ABC appearance and a team to support, the tax bill was larger than what he had paid in cash for that first house. The property tax on the little house ran $13,000 to $14,000 a year before the mortgage. A couple of years ago the listing price was $1.2 million. The house had not become a better house. The dollar had become a smaller dollar.

"The more you make, the more you pay in taxes. The more your house is worth, the more you pay in taxes. The less you have."

That is the line artists already know in their bodies. A good year does not accumulate. It gets skimmed. Abel had been told his whole life to save in dollars, and he had done it carefully, and the careful version still lost.

He looked at Bitcoin in 2016 and 2017. He did not bet the house on it. He rented, and he put a little in, and he left it alone. He has zapped people. He has not spent the position down. The argument he kept hearing was volatility. Bitcoin moves. Silver and gold and the dollar, he points out, have all been cut in half too. The difference is what the money can do when the ordinary rails fail. In Austin he knows programmers who can move sats without asking a bank for permission. He calls Bitcoin the closest thing he has found to a people's money.

On the episode he says what that changed about a working day. Earning in a melting unit is a race. You feel guilty picking up the guitar, because the people around you cannot see how a song pays the bills. A savings you do not have to defend every quarter gives the day back. He can write. He can practice. He has a recurring dream that he has died with songs still unplayed, and he wakes up glad, and he writes. That is the use of sound money for an artist. Not a ticker. Time.

## The rails were already there

Abel has been making music since before the words mattered to him. He remembers the feeling in his chest before he remembers the melody. A shy kid with a woodwind, watching a face light up, and learning that this was how he was useful to other people.

At Dartmouth he studied why music is everywhere when it does not help us eat or survive. His short answer is the one he gives in the episode. Music is a primitive kind of communication, almost purely emotional, baked into living things. Words let humans do extraordinary things. They also let us lose the heart. Done the human way, music is how a room goes through tension and release together. He told Dion, who danced for sixteen years before he founded Bitcoin For The Arts, that you cannot separate that from dance. The music starts and the body wants to move. The mistake is the label. "I'm a dancer." "I'm not a singer." Abel's line is that we are all of these things, and that a life gets smaller when we refuse ourselves the child's permission to try.

He took that same refusal into the music business.

Labels offered him deals. Television did. Publishers did. He read them. A deal is a sale, not a gift. The thing being sold can be the songs, the whole catalog, the name, the face, and the right to decide what the artist is allowed to mean on stage. He has watched talented people sign that away and come back unrecognizable, or back out and find themselves finished in the industry that just owned them. He will collaborate. He will not hand over his speech or his likeness.

He also said the quiet part about books. On a lot of big nonfiction, the name on the cover is not the person who wrote the sentences. The product is an illusion. He called it fiat. A fake artist, easy to market, no longer in charge of the work.

His own records went the other direction. Swamp Thing was made in 2015 with Denny Hemingson, bandleader and guitarist for Tim McGraw, with bassist David Santos and singer Wendy Moten. Abel sells Headline, the new one, directly. No label in the middle. For years Swamp Thing did what records do on the old platforms. It was heard. It paid pennies. He did not know the listeners, and they could not reach him.

About two years ago he put it on Wavlake and Fountain. A few people sent sats. The value-for-value DJs played it. More listeners found it. In a few weeks, hundreds of dollars came in on a record that had been earning almost nothing, plus messages he could answer. Dion heard him live first, at Nostr Vegas, and Abel talks about songs landing 150,000 sats while he was still playing, and about missing a 200,000-sat zap because he was inside the song and the room was the thing cheering.

The number he lingers on is smaller than those.

Ten sats. Twenty-one sats. From someone who liked a lick on the saxophone. He has had millions of plays in the old system and zero contact with a human on the other end. Twenty-one sats with a sentence attached felt like the ground moving, because it meant a person was there.

"The rails are already there. It already works. All we need is more people to be interacting with it and using this technology. And then you don't need the middle management. You don't need the people controlling your message anymore."

That is the whole invitation. Not a new app to wait for. A rail that already clears, sat by sat, from the listener to the person who made the thing.

## What he would do with a grant

Every guest on Share Your Bitcoin Journey gets the same last question. If a micro-grant of $500 to $2,000 in bitcoin landed in your wallet tomorrow, what would it unlock?

Abel did not ask for a new guitar.

He promised himself a long time ago that money from the music goes straight back into the music. He likes paying other artists for their time. And he named the part of the business that rarely makes the interview. When you get up and play your own songs, you are usually the last person paid. The band gets paid. The room gets paid. The person who wrote the song and is standing there singing it eats last. Playing original music is, in his words, generally not a good business decision. Covers and private parties are a different math. The original song is the risk.

So the grant, for him, would pay the musicians who show up to play. It would turn one night of original music from a loss into a night the whole room could afford to be in.

That is a precise description of what a Bitcoin For The Arts grant is for. Not a prize for being promising. A tool that lets the person who made the work pay the people who make the work possible. The dresser. The player. The friend who brought a horn. The artist who would otherwise be last.

A gift in that range, settled in bitcoin to a wallet the artist controls, does not pass through a label, a publisher, or a platform that keeps 30 percent and pays pennies two years later. It arrives as sats. It stays sats. Twenty-one million, and not one more, is what keeps a gift like that from being quietly watered down while it waits to be spent.

## What the first twenty-one taught us

We did not start the show to hit a number. We started it because artists are trained in the work and left illiterate about the money, and because the people who will understand this fastest are the ones the old system pays last.

Twenty-one conversations in, the pattern is no longer a hunch.

The check that looks like success from the audience is often the thing that boxes the artist in. The contract that looks like a rescue is often a sale of the name. The good year gets taxed and inflated back down to a starting position. The listener wants to pay the artist and is given no clean way to do it. And when a clean way exists, people use it. They use it for ten sats. They use it for a zap in the middle of a solo. They use it because being in the exchange feels different from clapping at a screen.

We learned something else, and it is the part we want the people who fund this to hear.

The artists were never the hard part. They will tell the truth about the money if someone builds a room where that truth is the subject. Abel did. So did the twenty before him. The scarce thing is not testimony. The scarce thing is the time, the editing, the travel, the grant on the other side of the question, and the next artist who has not been asked yet.

There is a smaller milestone inside the big one, and it is easy to miss. Episodes of this show unlock on Fanfares for 21 sats. The same number. A listener pays the people who made the hour, not an ad network. Twenty-one artists. Twenty-one million bitcoin. Twenty-one sats to hear one of them tell the truth. The amounts look different. The principle is identical. A fixed unit. A direct payment. No one in the middle rewriting the terms.

Abel is a good person to close the season on, because he already lived both sides of the expert problem. He got sick following instructions. He got well by taking responsibility for his own body. Then he looked at his house, his tax bill, and his catalog, and he took the same responsibility for his money and his songs. Health and money met in the grocery store, where real food got priced like a luxury and the cheap calories turned out to be the expensive ones. He does not think you can talk about what people eat anymore without talking about the unit those prices are measured in. We agree. An artist who cannot afford a real meal and an artist who cannot afford to pay the band are caught in the same dilution.

He told anyone watching for the first time to open a Nostr account and look around, and to go listen to the musicians already on Wavlake and Fountain, because the talent in that world is real. Several of the people he named from a Nashville room, Ainsley Costello, Sarah Jade, Joe Martin, have already sat in this chair. The room is not a theory. It is a group of people who ask what you are making and how they can help, instead of asking who they can move out of the way.

If you are artist number 22, or 40, or the one who thinks this does not include your discipline, the chair is open. Dion says it on the episode, and we mean it. Come tell the story. The show exists so that a working artist never has to be the only one in the room who has noticed the money.

## What a patron does here

Season one is twenty-one artists who said yes. Season two is a decision about whether those conversations keep happening, and whether the last question on the show keeps having a real answer.

Here is the plain version of where a gift goes.

A micro-grant is $500 to $2,000, paid in bitcoin, to a working artist. In Abel's case, that money would not buy him a newer version of a tool he already owns. It would pay the people on the bandstand so an original song could be played without the singer going home last and lightest. Across the artists who apply, it becomes studio time, a train ticket to the gig, a set of lights, a month of rent that keeps a practice going. The artist receives it in a wallet they control.

The show is the other half. Recording these conversations, cutting them, and putting them where a listener can pay sats for them is how the next twenty-one find the door. A patron is not buying a thank-you. A patron is paying for the hour in which an artist tells the truth, and for the grant that proves someone was listening.
