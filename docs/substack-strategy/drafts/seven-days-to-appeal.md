# Seven Days to Appeal

**Bitcoin for the Arts · Substack draft · for publication week of May 5, 2026**

## TITLE

Seven Days to Appeal

## SUBTITLE

The NEA gave hundreds of arts organizations one week to fight for grants they'd already won. The lesson isn't political. It's about what kind of money we're using.

---

## BODY

There is a single sentence buried in the NEA's mass-cancellation emails last week that is worth sitting with.

> *"The recipient can appeal the decision within seven days."*

Seven days.

Not seven weeks. Not the rest of the fiscal year. Not the duration of the grant period that, in many cases, the recipient organization had already begun spending against. Seven days to appeal a federal grant cancellation that arrived on a Friday night, from a generic `arts.gov` address, with no signatory and no phone number.

That sentence is the entire story. Not the politics around it. Not the question of whether the National Endowment for the Arts should exist, or what its priorities should be, or which administration is in power. Those are real questions, and other people are equipped to debate them.

The story we want to tell at Bitcoin for the Arts is simpler and older than any election: **money that can be revoked is not the same kind of money as money that cannot.** Every artist, every organization, every grant program in this country needs to learn that distinction now, while there is still time to plan for it.

---

## What Happened

On the evening of Friday, May 2, 2025, hundreds of grant recipients across the United States — large institutions, regional theaters, after-school art programs, community arts spaces, individual fellowships — opened emails informing them their NEA grants had been "withdrawn and terminated."

The list of organizations affected reads like a directory of American cultural infrastructure. Berkeley Repertory Theater, one of the most respected regional theaters in the country, lost funding. Central Park SummerStage, the free outdoor concert series that has been a fixture of New York summers for forty years, lost funding. Cornerstone Theater Company in Los Angeles lost a $40,000 grant for a project investigating the relationship between American theater and democracy — a project the company believed aligned with the NEA's stated new priority of celebrating "the 250th anniversary of American independence." The NEA disagreed. Studio Two Three, a community arts space in Richmond, Virginia, lost a $30,000 grant they had spent days writing, had been formally selected and approved for, and had received an acceptance letter for. The grant was rescinded retroactively. Open Studio Project in Chicago lost a two-year grant supporting visual art programs for elementary school students.

The cancellations arrived hours after the Trump administration submitted its FY2026 discretionary budget request, which formally proposed eliminating the NEA — along with the National Endowment for the Humanities and the Institute of Museum and Library Services — and explained that any "remaining funds" would "account for costs of orderly shutdowns."

The agency's emails redirected attention to the new priorities of the administration. Funding henceforth would focus on "projects that elevate the Nation's HBCUs and Hispanic Serving Institutions, celebrate the 250th anniversary of American independence, foster AI competency, empower houses of worship to serve communities, assist with disaster recovery, foster skilled trade jobs, make America healthy again, support the military and veterans, support Tribal communities, make the District of Columbia safe and beautiful, and support the economic development of Asian American communities."

This list is not the point of this article. Reasonable people disagree about which causes deserve federal funding, and that disagreement is what democracies are for. The point is that **the funding mechanism itself is no longer reliable** — and that the people building tomorrow's arts infrastructure should plan accordingly.

## Seven Days

Read the cancellation language one more time.

*"We are terminating awards that fall outside these new priorities … the recipient can appeal the decision within seven days."*

Imagine you are the executive director of a small theater company. Your board has approved a season built around a grant you secured eighteen months ago. You've hired a director. You've contracted a costume designer. You've signed a venue rental agreement. You've sent a press release. Your subscribers, your donors, your marketing partners — all of them are operating on the assumption that this season is happening, on this budget, with this funding.

It is 9 PM on a Friday in May. You open your email.

You have seven calendar days to mount a legal appeal of a grant termination from an agency that does not respond to comment requests, with no individual signatory on the letter, against a budget proposal explicitly framing your work as outside the priorities of the executive branch. The seven days include a weekend. They include the time it takes to find a lawyer who will work for arts-organization rates. They include the time it takes to read the appeal procedure, gather documentation, write a response, and submit it to a generic email address.

Seven days is not a deadline for appeal. It is a deadline designed not to be met.

This is not a comment on the legality of the cancellations. Courts will sort out whether the federal government can revoke awarded grants for political alignment reasons, and there will be a long fight there with smart attorneys on both sides. That fight will take years.

The fight that matters for individual artists and individual organizations, right now, is the one happening on the calendar in their kitchen: how do you plan a season, a project, a career, around money that can be revoked on seven days' notice with no human signature?

You don't. You can't. Nobody can. And that is the structural failure we should all be looking at.

## Money That Can Be Revoked

There is a phrase Bitcoin people use that sounds nerdy until it isn't: *finality*.

When a Bitcoin transaction is confirmed, it is final. There is no "withdrawal and termination" email. There is no seven-day appeal window. There is no generic agency address that will arrive on a Friday night to tell you the value you received eighteen months ago no longer aligns with new priorities. The transaction has been settled, by an irreversible mathematical process distributed across hundreds of thousands of independent computers around the world, and that finality is the entire point.

Federal arts grants, by contrast, are subject to a kind of conditional finality that is increasingly indistinguishable from no finality at all. The grant is "yours" until an administration decides it isn't. The award letter is binding until a generic email address says otherwise. The check has been deposited and the staff has been hired and the work has begun, and yet the funding can be retroactively pulled by an entity that does not pick up the phone.

This is a category of money distinct from money that cannot be revoked. It would be foolish to pretend the two are the same. They behave differently. They produce different outcomes. They support different kinds of work.

For sixty years, American arts infrastructure has been built on the first kind. Boards have planned around it. Foundations have matched against it. Marketing materials have promised it. Artists have moved cities for it. The assumption underlying nearly every nonprofit arts strategy in the country is that money awarded by a federal agency will, under normal circumstances, be paid out as awarded.

That assumption is now demonstrably wrong. It was always wrong, in a deeper sense — federal funding has always been subject to political shifts, and grants have been cut and reduced and reprioritized in every administration. But the brittleness has been disguised by the relative stability of the postwar consensus around arts funding, which kept the NEA reasonably insulated from the political weather even when individual administrations questioned its existence.

That consensus is gone. The weather is in the building.

The Trump administration's proposed elimination of the NEA from the FY2026 budget, the second consecutive year an elimination has been proposed, makes clear that the previous bipartisan deference is not a stable feature of the political landscape. State arts agencies are projected to see a 7.7% decrease in legislative appropriations in FY2026. Twenty-one states are projecting decreases. California is projecting a 40.8% drop in state arts funding. Missouri, 59.7%. Minnesota, 22%.

This is not a story about one administration. This is a story about the structural reliability of money that can be voted away.

## What Bitcoin Actually Solves

Let us be honest about what Bitcoin is and is not.

Bitcoin is not a substitute for the National Endowment for the Arts. It cannot, by itself, fund $207 million in annual grants to thousands of arts organizations across every congressional district in America. It cannot replace the policy infrastructure, the peer-review systems, the legitimating function that an established federal arts agency provides. It would be ridiculous to pretend it could.

Bitcoin is also not a political response to the cancellations. We are not building Bitcoin for the Arts as a partisan instrument. Our 501(c)(3) charter prohibits political activity, and we mean it. The artists we fund will include artists whose politics differ from ours, from each other's, and from the people who will read this article. That is the whole point. Art that only speaks to one tribe is not art that survives.

What Bitcoin does solve, and solves cleanly, is the *finality problem*. A grant paid in Bitcoin is paid. A donation received in Bitcoin is received. A reserve held in Bitcoin is held. There is no agency that can email you on a Friday night and reverse the transaction. There is no administration that can reprioritize the funds out from under a project that has already begun. There is no seven-day appeal window because there is nothing to appeal — the money is in your wallet, and it remains in your wallet until you choose to spend it.

This is a smaller claim than people often make about Bitcoin, and it is the claim worth making. Bitcoin will not save the arts in any totalizing sense. The arts do not need to be saved by a single instrument. They need to be reinforced by *additional* instruments that are structurally different from the legacy ones. The legacy infrastructure failing should make us less willing to depend on it as the primary source of cultural funding, and more willing to build parallel systems that fail differently.

Bitcoin grants fail differently. That is the entire offering.

## What We Are Building, Specifically

Bitcoin for the Arts is a 501(c)(3) nonprofit, registered in the State of New York, EIN 41-2642260. We are paying working artists directly in Bitcoin, in micro-grants ranging from $500 to $2,000 in our current funding round. The grants are settled to the artist's wallet within hours of approval, paid in BTC, with no possibility of revocation by any board, agency, or administration.

Every donation flows through a public allocation rule we call **55/30/10/5**:

- **55%** is paid out as direct artist grants
- **30%** funds programs — workshops, residencies, co-productions
- **10%** funds operations
- **5%** is held in long-term Bitcoin reserve, the **HODL Vault**

The HODL Vault is the part of this model that takes the longest to explain and is the most important. Every other arts funding mechanism in the United States is denominated in a currency designed to lose purchasing power over time. Even when an endowment gains in nominal terms, the dollars it holds buy less art, fewer programs, and shorter residencies each year. Inflation is the slow tax that arts foundations pay simply for existing through time.

A Bitcoin reserve does the opposite. Its supply is fixed at 21 million coins, forever, by mathematical rule. As demand for that fixed supply grows over decades — as it has, demonstrably, since Bitcoin's invention in 2009 — the purchasing power of the reserve grows with it. An endowment that strengthens against inflation rather than weakening against it is a different kind of financial instrument than the ones the arts have historically had access to. It is, at minimum, worth experimenting with.

We are paying every grant on-chain or via the Lightning Network, the layer-2 payment system that makes small Bitcoin payments fast and inexpensive. We publish our governance documents, our bylaws, our quarterly board minutes, and our grant terms openly on our website. Our grant program is open to applicants worldwide, with the requirement that the funded project produce a public benefit within the United States — by presenting, performing, exhibiting, distributing, teaching, or otherwise delivering work to U.S. audiences or communities.

The artist does not need to be a U.S. resident or citizen. The underlying work may have been created anywhere in the world. We will not be in a position to email anyone on a Friday night informing them that their funded project no longer aligns with new priorities, because we do not have those priorities. The priorities are the ones the artists bring.

## What This Week Should Teach Us

The arts community will spend the next several months arguing about politics. That is reasonable. Decades of cultural funding norms are being broken in real time, and the people whose work and livelihoods are at stake have every right to make that fight visible.

But while that argument is happening, the structural fact remains: every arts organization in America is now learning, the hard way, that the funding it depends on can be revoked on seven days' notice by a generic email. Whatever you think of the politics, that is now the operating reality. It will not be undone by a different administration in 2029. The norm has been broken; even if a future administration restores the agency to its previous role, no future board can plan around federal arts funding with the same confidence as before. That confidence was the foundation of fifty years of arts strategy. It is gone.

What replaces it cannot only be a different version of the same brittleness. Foundations whose grants can be retroactively revoked are subject to the same failure mode. Corporate sponsorships that depend on quarterly earnings reports are subject to a related failure mode. Even ticket revenue, the most local form of arts funding, depends on a discretionary-spending economy that is itself subject to monetary instability and recession.

The arts need durable funding instruments. Some of those instruments will continue to be the ones that have always existed — government, foundations, individual donors, corporate sponsorship, ticket sales. We are not arguing those should disappear. We are arguing that, in addition to those, the sector needs at least one funding mechanism whose finality is structural rather than political. That is the case for Bitcoin in the arts, narrowly stated.

Hundreds of arts organizations did not need an article to tell them that legacy funding is brittle. They opened the email Friday night.

We are building the alternative now, in real time, in public, on rails that cannot be revoked. If you are an artist whose grant was cut, an organization whose budget assumed money that no longer arrives, or a donor watching the whole system wobble and looking for a place to send capital that will outlast this cycle — we are here.
