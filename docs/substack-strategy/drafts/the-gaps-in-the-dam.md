## TITLE

The Gaps in the Dam

## SUBTITLE

Chicago’s Dance for Life benefit is on track to raise $500,000 for dancers who are uninsured, underinsured, and aging off their parents’ plans. The story is right about healthcare. Here is the chapter about money that dancers were never taught.

---

## BODY

On August 20, 2026, WBEZ published a story that every working dancer in America already knows in their body and almost never sees named in print with this much clarity.

Erica Thompson’s piece follows contemporary ballet dancer Nia Davis of Visceral Dance Chicago as she rehearses for Dance for Life, the Chicago Dance Health Fund’s thirty-fifth benefit at The Auditorium. The show is nearly sold out. It is on track to raise half a million dollars. Eleven local companies will share a stage. A world-premiere finale by Randy Duncan will close the night. The money will go to Critical Health Needs Grants of up to $5,000 and General Health and Wellness Grants of $1,000, plus partnerships that get dancers into free or discounted care.

Davis teaches. She performs. She juggles jobs because the art alone does not pay the rent. Earlier this year she fractured her foot and got treated quickly as a dependent on her parents’ insurance. In November she turns twenty-six. After that, coverage is an open question her employers do not answer.

“I have no idea,” she said. “Hopefully it comes to me with ease.”

Phill Wood of Chicago Dance Crash used a fund grant to afford a therapist for the first time. “This money saves lives,” he said. Mekeba Malik of Deeply Rooted Dance Theater paid medical bills after a foot injury with the same help. Anais Bueno of the Joffrey Ballet watched a colleague’s cancer treatment become survivable in part because the fund showed up when the invoices did.

James Odom, a regional director of the American Guild of Musical Artists, named the structural truth without flinching:

> “Healthcare is a problem the nation has to solve, and what we’re doing now is filling in the gaps in the dam before it collapses from the leaks. That’s not sustainable.”

Every word of that reporting is correct.

Bitcoin for the Arts is not here to argue with the Chicago Dance Health Fund. We are here because Odom’s metaphor is the whole story, and the article, like almost every arts-labor story in American media, stops one layer short of naming what is making the dam crack faster every year.

## What the article gets right

Dancers are underinsured. Many companies cannot afford plans. The ones that can are often the larger, better-funded houses. Smaller modern companies that somehow still provide coverage are doing something heroic and rare. AGMA organizing is rising for a reason. Mental health support is not a luxury for people whose jobs are their bodies. Grants for physical therapy, dental work, cancer care, and counseling are not “nice to have.” They are triage.

The Chicago Dance Health Fund began as an AIDS-era benefit for AIDS Foundation Chicago and evolved into a broader healthcare lifeline. That history matters. Dance communities have always built mutual aid when institutions failed them. Dance for Life is that tradition, still alive, still raising half a million dollars in a single night.

If you are a Chicago dancer, apply. If you are a donor who can underwrite the fund, do. If you are in another city without an equivalent, notice the hole.

This article is not a substitute for that work. It is a completion of the frame around it.

## The sentence dancers are never taught

Here is what stopped us on the first read.

In a piece about dancers who cannot afford therapy, who age off parental insurance at twenty-six, who downplay injuries because the bill is scarier than the pain, who balance enough work to survive without earning so much that they lose Medicaid or subsidy eligibility, the reporting names healthcare costs, underfunded companies, union limits, and federal complexity.

It never once names *inflation* as a monetary event.

It never once names *purchasing power*.

It never once asks why a $1,000 wellness grant buys less care every year, or why company insurance premiums rise faster than ticket revenue, or why a dancer’s side-hustle wages feel like they are running uphill on a down escalator.

That is not Erica Thompson’s failure. That is the water American culture swims in. Newspapers write about “rising healthcare costs” as if costs rise by themselves. Trade publications write about “underfunded arts organizations” as if the underfunding is a weather pattern. Dancers are taught turnout, repertory, audition etiquette, and how to ice a tendon. They are almost never taught how the currency they are paid in loses value across a career.

Bitcoin for the Arts exists to close that specific educational gap. Not to turn dancers into traders. Not to pretend Bitcoin is a health plan. To make dancers into people who can see what is happening to their wages, their premiums, their grants, and their savings between contracts.

## Why the dam leaks faster every season

Start with the body economics every dancer already lives.

A professional dancing life is a short, high-depreciation career. Peak earning years are often the same years the body is absorbing the most damage. Benefits, when they exist, are seasonal, company-specific, or contingent on enough contracted weeks to qualify. Many dancers stitch together teaching, gig work, commercial jobs, and unpaid rehearsals. At twenty-six, parental coverage ends. At thirty, deductibles still feel theoretical until the first MRI. At thirty-five, the question is not only “Am I hired?” It is “Can I afford to stay hireable?”

Now put that body economy inside a monetary economy that expands.

When the money supply grows faster than the real goods and services it can buy, every dollar buys less. Healthcare is one of the sectors where this shows up earliest and hardest. Premiums, specialty care, imaging, physical therapy, dental, and mental health out-of-pocket costs do not politely wait for arts organizations to catch up. They reprice. Companies that already run on thin seasons face insurance renewals they cannot meet. Dancers who buy coverage on the Affordable Care Act watch premiums and deductibles move. A Critical Health Needs Grant that felt like a bridge in 2019 is a shorter bridge in 2026 if the invoice on the other side grew faster than the grant.

This is why Odom is right that gap-filling is not sustainable.

The leaks are not only moral or managerial. They are arithmetic. Arts organizations are trying to buy yesterday’s care with tomorrow’s diluted dollars, while dancers try to store surplus (when there is any) in the same unit of account that is quietly losing ground underneath them.

Stopgap grants remain necessary. They also remain stopgaps. Universal healthcare, if and when the country ever builds it, would change the floor. Until then, dancers still need a way to understand the money system that is pricing them out of their own maintenance.

## What Bitcoin is not (read this twice)

Bitcoin is not health insurance.

Bitcoin will not replace the Chicago Dance Health Fund.

Bitcoin will not pay your deductible by magic.

Bitcoin will not substitute for AGMA contracts, company plans, Medicaid, or the ACA.

Anyone selling you that story is not doing mission work. They are doing marketing.

What Bitcoin can be, for a working dancer, is narrower and more honest:

1. **An education in how money works.** Why your rent, your Metro pass, your PT co-pay, and your company budget all move in the same direction even when nobody in the studio “got greedy.”
2. **A savings rail between contracts.** If you manage to put anything aside, the question is whether that savings is designed to sit still or designed to melt. Sound money is not a personality. It is a tool for people whose income is lumpy and whose careers are finite.
3. **A payment rail for mutual aid and micro-support.** Lightning tips, peer-to-peer support, and nonprofit micro-grants can move to an artist in hours, not grant-cycle months, when a body needs care now.
4. **A way for arts funders to preserve purchasing power across time.** A fund that holds long-term reserves only in a diluting unit of account is volunteering to buy less care every year with the same nominal dollars.

That fourth point is where Bitcoin for the Arts lives as a 501(c)(3). We pay artists in Bitcoin when they opt in. We teach monetary literacy the conservatory skipped. We are building reserves meant to fund creative work without assuming the dollar will kindly hold still.

We do not claim this solves American healthcare. We claim dancers deserve the monetary chapter their industry never assigned.

## The education Phill Wood asked for

Wood told WBEZ that dancers can benefit from more education on how to apply for health insurance. He is thirty and still “figuring out basic things to get by in society.”

That sentence should haunt every conservatory dean in the country.

Add one more class to the list.

Teach dancers what a premium, deductible, and out-of-pocket maximum are. Teach them how open enrollment works. Teach them what happens at twenty-six. Then teach them why those numbers keep rising in the first place. Teach them what inflation is as an expansion of the money supply, not merely as “prices went up.” Teach them that a grant denominated in dollars is a claim on future care that may cost more than the claim can buy. Teach them that organizing for benefits and learning sound money are not opposites. They are two forms of self-determination.

Odom said dancers are becoming “far better educated on taking care of themselves,” and that organizing gives them “some sense of self-determination in the workplace.”

Financial self-determination belongs in that same sentence.

## Holding a lot of things in

Davis said there are a lot of dancers holding a lot of things in. That they try so hard they forget the joy.

Every dancer who has ever iced a joint in a dressing room, taught a morning class on a bad night’s sleep, smiled through a tech rehearsal while calculating rent, or waited until the contract ended to admit an injury felt that line in their sternum.

Healthcare gaps are not only an insurance story. They are a silence story. Fear of the bill becomes fear of the diagnosis. Fear of the diagnosis becomes a shorter career. A shorter career becomes a lost teacher, a lost choreographer, a lost elder in the room who would have held the next generation.

The Chicago Dance Health Fund recognizes the value dancers bring to Chicago. That recognition is love with a check attached. It deserves celebration on August 22 and every year after.

Bitcoin for the Arts is asking the adjacent question:

What would it mean to recognize dancers’ value not only at the emergency room door, but in the monetary system that decides how expensive that door becomes?

## Completing the story

Dance for Life fills gaps in the dam. It should. Keep filling them.

AGMA organizes for workplace self-determination. It should. Keep organizing.

Companies that still offer insurance are doing rare, expensive, moral work. Protect that.

And for the dancers who will age off a parent’s plan this year, who will meet a deductible for the first time, who will choose between the MRI and the rent, who will receive a $1,000 wellness grant and stretch it like gospel:

Learn how money works.

Learn why the leaks accelerate.

Learn that you are not bad with money because your career is seasonal. You were trained in an industry that discusses everything except the unit of account.

Bitcoin may or may not become part of your personal stack. That choice is yours. The literacy is not optional anymore.

The nation still has to solve healthcare. Odom is right.

Until it does, dancers will keep dancing on a leaking dam.

The fundraisers will keep patching.

The invoices will keep arriving.

Someone has to teach the field what water pressure is.

That is the story WBEZ opened.

This is the chapter we are here to write.
