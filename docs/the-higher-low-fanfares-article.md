## From the Ramones to Law & Order

It started on the first day of high school. His best friend stopped him in the corridor: we have a band, and you are the keyboard player. They played the big, complicated songs. Then, at sixteen, they saw the Ramones in Helsinki.

"After the show we just looked at each other. Okay, we're done with this complicated stuff. We're going to do really simple, really direct, melodic, energetic music for the rest of our lives."

That band became Fumble. Their first single, *Walk Alone*, came out in 1992. Within a year they were playing festivals and opening for their heroes. They took a break in 1994, came back in 2006, and used the internet to book their own tours through Sweden, Germany, and beyond. One of their songs now opens an episode of *Law & Order: SVU*, which still airs every week somewhere. "Still our most famous song, even though we never played it live."

Thirty years with the same friends gave him a sixth sense on stage, and one rule: "Even if there's two people and a dog at a coffee shop, we still play like it's a thousand people at a crazy rock festival."

## A borrowed guitar and a rabbit hole

He had wanted a solo project for years. Songs he wrote while living in New York and San Francisco ended up as Fumble songs once he moved home. Then work started sending him on two-week trips to the Middle East. The weekends were empty, and the apartment had a guitar.

He was deep in the Bitcoin rabbit hole by then. He picked up the guitar and wrote *LFG* on the spot. More songs followed. Over one Christmas break, a fever kept him off a group trip to Estonia. He woke up the next day feeling fine, with everyone already gone, so he took his laptop to the practice room and recorded all twelve songs in a single four-hour session.

A Bitcoin community heard the early releases, invited him onto a Spaces conversation, and then asked him to play an event in Boston. He needed a set, so he picked the ten best songs and released them as *Node To Freedom* in February 2023. The title is a play on ABBA's "Ode to Freedom."

## Why the songs never say Bitcoin

He set the rule the moment The Higher Low existed. The songs should speak about life in a digital world and carry more than one meaning. Someone five years into Bitcoin hears exactly what he means. Someone who has never heard of it can still find their own story in the song.

Even the name works that way. A higher low is a trading-chart term, but to him it means more:

"Your life will never be a straight line. There's always going to be ups and downs. But if you work hard and learn from mistakes, then those lows are hopefully a little less low in the future."

He also wants to carry an older spirit. To him, 2017 to around 2021 was a golden era of Bitcoin culture, before treasury companies and the talk of yield. "It was all about freedom technology. Nobody understands us. We just need to work hard, build great tools, and help each other." That is the culture he writes for.

## The pyramid

He heard about Bitcoin while working in the US, but it took a colleague in 2016, with one screen for code and one for the Bitcoin price, to make him pay attention. He bought a little in 2017 and more at $3,000. In early 2020 he read *The Bitcoin Standard*. "That was it. Bitcoin is the thing. I lost interest in everything else."

He sees the same shape in music and money. "Unless you're Taylor Swift, you can't make any money from streaming. Unless you're at the top of the pyramid, you don't make any money." The fiat system works the same way.

Value for value is the way out. Fans send sats straight to the artist. It is early, and nobody lives on it yet, but this year, at well-run shows in Las Vegas and elsewhere, artists received meaningful amounts. And it is the same thing Fumble did in the nineties: self-released records and self-booked tours. "That felt like punk rock to me."

## The campfire test

His best songs arrive in one go, often after weeks away from the guitar. "Chords, melodies, lyrics, everything comes literally in three minutes." That happens a couple of times a year. For everything else, thirty years of writing lets him throw out the weak ideas fast. Too much like a Green Day song. Too ordinary. Keep playing.

He compares it to photography. You can edit a picture all you want, but if the original is not good, you cannot fix it afterwards. "If four chords, an acoustic guitar, and one guy singing already sounds good, then when you start layering drums and electric guitars, it's much more likely to sound great in the end."

Playing alone pushed him out of his comfort zone. For decades he had been the frontman with a mic in his hand. With just a guitar, there is nothing to hide behind. "In the last four years I learned more as a musician than in the previous ten."

## Opening the room

At Bitcoin conferences he is usually the opener, one man with a guitar and the least volume in the building, in a room full of people already talking about Bitcoin. His job is to win them over in thirty to forty minutes, bring them closer to the stage song by song, and have everyone singing the chorus by the end.

His favorite room is Baltic Honeybadger in Riga, about six hundred people. In 2024 he played *Freedom Engine* there for the first time, fast, almost punk tempo. Mid-song, writer and musician Knut Svanholm jumped on stage, sat at the drums, and played it slower. Rami matched him. The slower version stuck.

## Freedom In the Digital Age

For the second album he brought the songs to his Fumble friends and the Helsinki scene. He handed over the core: lyrics, melodies, chords, played on acoustic guitar. Then he said nothing and let the band decide how it would sound. Guitarist and producer Jesperi Mommo "understands the soul of a song" and turned the acoustic sketches into big rock songs.

*Freedom Engine* was written in El Zonte, El Salvador, on his way to a Bitcoin halving party in 2024. The night before, local Bitcoiners threw a networking party. He woke up inspired, picked up the guitar, and the song came out whole. "It's almost like a postcard for myself."

The album launched on August 15, 2025, with a full-band show at BTChel in Helsinki. The vinyl was crowdfunded on Geyser.

He has never spent the sats his music earns. "I just keep them somewhere. Bitcoin keeps going up, and one day it's going to be enough to do another album."

## The next five years

Fewer shows, better shows. More singles, the occasional EP or album. Fumble is still playing festivals in Finland. By day he runs an AI transformation agency from the Middle East, and he still finds the time. "Sometimes if I have twenty minutes, I take my guitar, rehearse a couple of songs, try some new ideas. It compounds over time."

## What a micro-grant would unlock

We ask every guest the same last question: what would a $500 to $2,000 Bitcoin micro-grant from Bitcoin For The Arts unlock?

Two things. He keeps getting invited to play far from home, in Japan, Australia, Brazil, and Argentina, and has never been able to justify the cost himself. And his new songs are ready to record. Working with Jesperi, "even with $500 I could do five songs, really high-quality production."

Five songs that could outlive him, for $500 in Bitcoin. That is what this kind of support does.
