The Dispatch  ·  Anniversary Week

One year. Twenty-one artists.

Dear sovereign renaissance,

This is our anniversary week. On Sunday, November 16, 2025, three trustees met on a video call and adopted our bylaws. On November 24, 2025, our certificate of incorporation was filed with New York State, and Bitcoin for the Arts became a real organization. We will come back to that year at the end.

First, a new episode with a number in it that we could not have planned, a class for anyone who is starting from zero, and an honest look at a hard week for people who work in the arts.

   

◆ Share Your Bitcoin Journey  ·  Episode 21

Twenty-one is the number the money stops at.

Episode 21 is out. Our guest is Abel James. He is a musician, a New York Times bestselling author, and the host of The Fat-Burning Man podcast, which he has made for more than fifteen years. He studied brain science and music at Dartmouth, and he has turned down deals that asked him to hand over his voice or his likeness along with his work.

On the episode, Abel talks about a record that paid pennies on the old platforms for years and found real listeners once he put it where fans could pay him directly. The moment he keeps coming back to is small. Twenty-one sats, with a sentence attached, from someone who liked a saxophone lick. After millions of plays with no contact with a single listener, it meant a person was there.

His line, for any artist who thinks they need permission: "The rails are already there. It already works."

Twenty-one artists. Twenty-one ways in.

Abel is also the 21st artist to share a story on this show, and that matters to us. There will only ever be 21 million bitcoin. We did not choose twenty-one artists for a poster. It is the number the money itself stops at, and a small count on purpose: small enough to finish, small enough that every person was truly heard.

Look at who sat in that chair. A Royal Ballet soloist who discovered Bitcoin while negotiating dancer contracts. A Puerto Rican filmmaker who walked away from a career producing for Google, Apple, and Volkswagen to make the documentary Dirty Coin. A Mississippi mother who rebuilt her family's life on a Bitcoin standard after losing her husband. An Alaskan digital artist who used to compete at the X Games. A Finnish punk singer who writes songs about Bitcoin that never say the word. A painter in the mountains of central Italy. A designer from Sydney. A maker who brands with steel, fire, and leather. A performer who came to Bitcoin through GameStop and AMC. And Ainsley Costello, a Nashville pop-rock artist who was the first on Wavlake to earn a million sats.

They are dancers, musicians, painters, filmmakers, writers, woodcarvers, and designers, from Helsinki to San Juan, from Alaska to London to Austin. No two came to Bitcoin the same way, and no two understand it the same way. For some it is freedom from gatekeepers. For some it is sound money that finally lets them save. For one it is an "energy agreement." For another it is a payment rail that lets a fan say thank you with a sentence attached. One of them titled his episode "Bitcoin Saved My Life."

Nobody handed them a lesson plan. Each found the part of Bitcoin that matched the way they already see the world, and built from there. That is the point of the series, and of our class. There is no single right way in. There is yours.

On the stage  ·  An update

No deal yet. No strike yet.

This week we published No Crew, No Show, about the contract fight between Broadway's backstage unions and the Broadway League. Here is where it stands this Monday morning, based on the unions' statements and published reports.

Stagehands, IATSE Local One. The old contract expired July 26. Talks ended at about 2:30 AM last Wednesday with no agreement, resumed Thursday, and more sessions are set for today and tomorrow. The union says it has dropped every proposal except wage and benefit increases, and that the League has reduced its list but still wants major changes to working conditions. Members authorized a strike unanimously in October, and the union's international president has now allowed the local to call one if needed. None has been called, and there is no announced timeline.

Wardrobe, IATSE Local 764. This is the new development. On Sunday night, members voted unanimously to authorize a strike. They have worked without a contract since July 26. The union says the League canceled sessions set for November 18 and 19, and it wants new dates right away. Pay is the dividing line. On the union's figures, a wardrobe department head still has to work nearly 45 percent longer than a comparable department head to earn the same pay, and the local says it will not accept a deal that widens that gap.

A strike authorization is a permission slip, not a walkout. As of this morning, the lights are still on. In the reports we have seen, the Broadway League has not commented publicly.

We do not know the terms and we are not taking a side. What we said last week still holds. Everyone at that table is up against the same rising prices, and it is very hard to decide who should be devalued more or less. If you are a performer who works next to these crews, check in on someone this week.

   

The wider picture

The strain is not only on Broadway.

We keep reading the same story from different places, so we are going to keep reporting it.

New Jersey. Appel Farm, an arts camp and education nonprofit that has served young people for 66 years, announced last Thursday that it is closing. Its board cited an unrecoverable funding deficit, with giving from individuals, corporations, and foundations down and utility and insurance costs up. Every employee was let go. In its last reported year it took in about $2.3 million and spent about $2.6 million. (The Philadelphia Inquirer)

Minnesota. The Science Museum of Minnesota announced it will cut or restructure 22 positions and end its touring exhibitions program to close a $4.64 million shortfall. It is the museum's second round of layoffs in two years. Attendance is about half of what it was in 2019, and the museum points to steep reductions in federal and state funding. (Minnesota Star Tribune)

New Zealand. Little Andromeda, an independent theatre in Christchurch where 75 percent of ticket sales go straight to the artists, will close at its current home after its final show on December 19. Its national arts funding went from $80,000 a year to zero in July. The team hopes to reopen in a new space and is raising money to do it. (Theatreview)

Screen actors. On Friday, SAG-AFTRA's national board voted unanimously to create a President's Healthcare Task Force. President Sean Astin said the union's health plan is "healthy and strong, but a substantial majority of our members do not qualify." In 2026 a member needs $28,090 in covered earnings, or 108 eligible workdays, to qualify. The earnings bar rises to $28,933 in 2027. Astin said that for members who do not qualify, there is very little that can be done to provide coverage. The task force will engage lawmakers, other unions, and coalitions, and push for universal access to affordable care. Actors have been saying it for weeks. In late October, the actor Cheyenne Jackson started a campaign called One Job Away, for performers who are one or a few jobs short of qualifying.

We are reporting what the union said. Bitcoin for the Arts does not take a position on legislation.

The details differ and the thread is familiar. Costs rise, funding and attendance fall, and the people who make the work carry the gap. Whatever happens in Washington, an artist whose income arrives in gigs needs savings that hold their value between them. That is why we keep teaching what money is.

   

◆ Year one

Low and steady.

A year ago, Bitcoin for the Arts was a set of bylaws and an idea. We will be honest: we do not have a long list of big numbers. What we have is a steady one. We stayed engaged. We stayed committed. We kept showing up, week after week, for artists who were never taught how money works.

Here is what that steadiness added up to:

21 artists have told their stories on Share Your Bitcoin Journey.

Bitcoin Arts Park opened at the Midwest Bitcoin Summit in Columbus, with artists on stage in front of the Bitcoin community.

Three artists were awarded micro-grants. Tap a name to see their work: Ainsley Costello, a Nashville musician; Aksana Zasinets, who makes hand embroidery; and Christopher Arcella, behind the feature film The Bitcoin Executor.

A free class for artists began this month.

35 issues of this letter, and a growing circle of artists, builders, and organizations in the Bitcoin ecosystem who have opened their stages and audiences to working artists.

In Bitcoin circles there is a phrase for how we have tried to work: low time preference. It means choosing the long game over the quick win. Saving instead of spending it all today. Building instead of flipping. It is the habit Bitcoin rewards, and it turns out to be the habit a young nonprofit needs, too. Our time preference is low and steady, like the Bitcoin ethos itself. Nothing about the last twelve months was loud. All of it compounds.

Year two is more of the same, on purpose: more artist stories, more classes, and Nashville in 2027. If you want to help us start it, monthly giving is the most patient way to do it. If you want to read where we began, our founding board minutes are public.

Thank you for being here for year one.

Onward.
