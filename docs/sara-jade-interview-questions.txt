Share Your Bitcoin Journey: Sara Jade — Interview Questions

For BFTA Podcast Series · YouTube · Recording date TBD
Show host: Dion Wilson · Founder, Executive Director, Interim Chair, Bitcoin for the Arts

Source material reviewed: sarajademusic.com · sarajademusic.com/epk · "It's A Mood"
podcast extended interview (May 2025) · SHOUTOUT SOCAL interview · The Bitcoin Vinyl
Record (Nashville 2024) · Heather Larson Substack on the Spotify "Never Knew"
takedown · Sara Jade's Nostr presence (30K+ followers).

Note for the host: Sara has done several podcast interviews already, including one
long-form on "It's A Mood" where she covered the Spotify "Never Knew" phantom-
streaming takedown story in detail. Don't rehash the basic biographical arc those
shows have covered. The angles BFTA should pull on that other interviewers
haven't fully mined: (1) her father's-band origins as an apprenticeship rather
than a career launching pad, (2) the keytar as a deliberate signature against
indie-rock conventions, (3) the Spotify takedown as policy-relevant evidence
of platform fragility, (4) Songwriter Sisters as a Bitcoin-adjacent community
model worth replicating, and (5) the bridge moment of opening for Jason Mraz
in April 2026 as a mainstream-vs-independent inflection point. The closing
question (Q21) is sacred — every episode of Share Your Bitcoin Journey ends
with the BFTA micro-grant prompt.


BACKGROUND & JOURNEY

1. Sara, welcome to Share Your Bitcoin Journey. For anyone meeting you here for
   the first time — you're a cinematic pop-rock artist out of San Diego, you
   play keytar, you won the 2026 San Diego Music Award for Best Pop Song, you
   opened for Jason Mraz in April, you've performed in Vegas multiple times,
   and you've built a 30,000-plus following on Nostr. That's a lot. Where does
   the story actually start for you, before any of that?

2. You cut your teeth as a vocalist in your father's band. That's an
   apprenticeship most working musicians never get — being inside a serious
   musical operation as a kid, hearing how songs get built, watching adults
   actually do this as a craft. What did being inside your dad's band teach
   you that you don't think you could've learned anywhere else?

3. There's a moment in every musician-from-a-musical-family story where they
   choose to forge their own path instead of inheriting one. When was that
   moment for you? What was the first thing that was yours and yours alone?

4. Your 2021 ballad "Jacoma" got a San Diego Music Award nomination — that
   was an Americana piece, much more intimate than what you make now. The
   cinematic pop-rock you're doing today is a real genre shift. Was that
   evolution driven by what you wanted to write, or was it the band that
   formed around you, or was it something else?

5. The keytar. It's a deliberate choice — most singer-songwriters fronting
   a rock band would pick a guitar or just a mic. You picked the most
   theatrical, most maligned, most "80s" instrument in pop music history
   and made it your signature. Tell us about that decision.


THE BITCOIN TURN

6. You're described on your own EPK as an "early adopter of the value-for-
   value music movement." Most independent musicians I talk to who eventually
   find Bitcoin came in through inflation, or libertarian politics, or a
   technical friend. What was your way in? What was the first crack in the
   wall for you?

7. You moved heavily onto Nostr — 30,000 plus followers is real scale in
   that ecosystem. Most musicians at your career level treat Nostr as an
   experiment alongside their main platforms. You leaned in. What did Nostr
   give you that Instagram, TikTok, and Spotify couldn't?

8. The value-for-value music model — for any listener who hasn't encountered
   it — describe what it actually means for an independent artist day to day.
   How does it change how you make decisions about what songs to release,
   what shows to play, what your relationship with your audience is?


SPOTIFY, STREAMING, AND THE INSTITUTIONAL BREAK

9. Let's talk about "Never Knew." Spotify pulled it. Their stated reason was
   phantom streaming — the algorithmic accusation that your own streams were
   somehow fraudulent. You're not the only artist this has happened to;
   you're not even close. But you talked about it publicly, on the "It's A
   Mood" podcast in May 2025, in a way most artists are too afraid to. What
   actually happened?

10. The phantom-streaming accusation is one of the most insulting things a
    platform can throw at an artist, because it's both: a) "your fans don't
    really love you," and b) "we're going to demonetize you for the
    affection your fans show." What did that experience do to your trust
    in centralized platforms, beyond just Spotify?

11. There's a policy story underneath your personal story here. Spotify
    pulling tracks for "phantom streams" is not an artist-protection
    measure. It's a platform-margin-management measure. Bitcoin and value-
    for-value don't have phantom streams — you're paid directly by listeners,
    per actual play, and nobody between you and them can revoke it. For an
    artist who hasn't lived this, articulate what changes when the rails
    underneath the music can't deplatform you.


THE MUSIC ITSELF

12. "Singin' Woman" — your 2024 track featuring Jessie Lark and Elizabeth
    Bowersox — was featured on The Bitcoin Vinyl Record at the Bitcoin
    Conference in Nashville. Tell us the story of that song. How did the
    three of you come together on it, and how did it end up on a literal
    Bitcoin vinyl record?

13. The cinematic pop-rock aesthetic — the spectacle, the immersive show,
    the "ringmaster drawing audiences into the spectacle" framing on your
    EPK — that's not how most singer-songwriters present themselves. It
    sounds closer to theater than to indie music. Where does that
    sensibility come from?

14. You opened for Jason Mraz in April 2026. That's a major stage and a
    mainstream music-industry moment. What was it like stepping into that
    arena straight from a career you've built largely outside the
    traditional industry?

15. You've performed at Bitcoin conferences, at NOSTR events, and now in
    venues that book traditional touring acts like Mraz. The audiences in
    those rooms are wildly different. What does the Bitcoin community
    audience get about your music that a traditional music audience doesn't,
    and vice versa?


SONGWRITER SISTERS

16. You founded Songwriter Sisters in 2024 — a community uplifting and
    amplifying women in music. And you created and produced San Diego's
    HalloWomen Spooktacular concert, female-fronted, theatrical, now an
    annual tradition that doubled in both scale and sponsorship between
    its first and second years. Where did the idea for that come from?

17. Women are underrepresented in indie rock, and they're severely
    underrepresented in the Bitcoin music community specifically. Songwriter
    Sisters is doing the slow work of changing the first; you're doing some
    of the most visible work of changing the second by being one of the very
    few women at this career level inside Bitcoin music. What do you think
    the path looks like — how do more women find their way in?

18. The HalloWomen Spooktacular doubled in scale and sponsorship from year
    one to year two. That kind of sponsorship growth in the independent
    music world is meaningful. Who's funding it, what's the structure, and
    is there a Bitcoin angle to how Songwriter Sisters might be funded
    going forward?


LOOKING FORWARD

19. The traditional music industry is structured around scarcity of
    distribution, scarcity of attention, scarcity of capital, and scarcity
    of access to gatekeepers. Bitcoin and value-for-value invert all four —
    distribution is open, attention is direct, capital is permissionless,
    and there are no gatekeepers. What does a music career architected from
    the ground up on those new rules actually look like five years from
    now? You're as well-positioned as anyone to describe it.

20. What's next for you in the back half of 2026 and into 2027 — new music,
    new tours, new Songwriter Sisters expansion? Where are you pointed?


THE CLOSING QUESTION

21. Last question — we ask every guest this on Share Your Bitcoin Journey:
    if a $500 to $2,000 Bitcoin micro-grant from Bitcoin for the Arts landed
    in your wallet tomorrow, what would it unlock for you as a creator?

NOTES ON ORDER

  - Q1-5 are biographical warmup. The keytar question (Q5) is fun and
    sets a personable tone before the conversation gets heavier. Spend ~10
    minutes max on this section.

  - Q6-8 are the Bitcoin turn. Let her describe the value-for-value model
    in her own words at Q8 — it's a concept many in the BFTA audience
    are still figuring out, and her articulation will be cleaner than
    yours could be.

  - Q9-11 are the policy/institutional break section. This is the heart
    of why her story matters beyond music — she has documented evidence
    of platform fragility that policy people, foundation officers, and
    journalists should be able to cite from her experience. Let her be
    angry if she wants to be; don't soften her.

  - Q12-15 are the music itself. Lighter section, but Q14 (the Mraz
    opener) is the bridge moment between independent and mainstream
    worlds — it gets more interesting if you push on the contrast.

  - Q16-18 are Songwriter Sisters. The women-in-Bitcoin-music conversation
    is one BFTA hasn't yet hosted seriously, and Sara is the right voice
    for it. Don't rush it.

  - Q19-21 are the wrap. Q21 is sacred — every episode ends with it.

If running over 60 minutes, the questions to consider cutting are Q4
(genre evolution — may be covered organically in other questions),
Q13 (theatrical aesthetic — interesting but can be glossed if time-
constrained), and Q18 (HalloWomen funding structure — worth its own
followup or a Songwriter Sisters profile newsletter piece). Q9-11 and
Q21 should never be cut.
