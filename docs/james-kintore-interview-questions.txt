Share Your Bitcoin Journey: James Kintore — Interview Questions


Format note (Dion only): Two beats per question. One short premise + one short
question. ~30–40 words total. Anything that doesn't fit the premise becomes a
follow-up after he answers. Same intellectual depth as the Benjamin Ella set;
just tighter delivery so the punch lands and James doesn't lose the thought.


HOST INTRO (spoken on camera before Q1)

Same structure as the Ethan Turer and Joe Martin episodes — constant opener,
then guest framing, then a one-line "in this conversation" tease, then the
handoff.

  Welcome to another episode of Share Your Bitcoin Journey presented by
  Bitcoin for the Arts. I am Dion Wilson, the founder and executive director
  for Bitcoin for the Arts, a 501(c)(3) nonprofit empowering artists with
  Bitcoin grants, education, and sovereign funding tools.

  Today, we have the pleasure of speaking with an Australian interior
  designer, the founder of Kintore Design, and one of the most respected
  voices in high-end residential design on the New South Wales coast. Known
  for his uncompromising materiality, his small-batch furniture work through
  his brand Mat and Kin, and his clear-eyed advocacy for sound money inside
  the design world.

  His Woollahra Residence project was published in The Local Project and
  Artedomus for the level of craft and intention in the work. He runs Mat
  and Kin out of walnut and cast-bronze pieces — small batch, made to outlast
  the buyer. He has spoken publicly about Bitcoin as a store of value
  alongside real estate, and brings the framework of "going where you're
  treated best" to how he thinks about life and work.

  In this conversation, he shares his personal journey from designer to
  Bitcoiner, how the materials he chooses for clients and the conviction he
  holds for Bitcoin point in the same direction — toward things made to last.
  We talk about what his high-net-worth design clients are actually saying
  about the Australian dollar in 2026, what a Bitcoin-native client of the
  future looks like, and what a design career on a Bitcoin standard could be.
  His story is grounded, articulate, and full of practical wisdom for any
  maker or craftsperson who has ever felt the pressure of fiat erosion on the
  work they were trying to make.

  Without further ado, welcome to the show, James Kintore.

1. James, your interior work — the Woollahra Residence in particular — got
picked up by The Local Project for the level of materiality and craft. For
listeners who haven't seen your studio yet, what is Kintore Design and what
kind of work do you put your name on?

2. Interior design is built on long time horizons — European oak, walnut, cast
bronze, materials that gain patina and outlast the client. When did Bitcoin
start to feel like the same logic at a different layer?

3. You closed the company entity at the start of 2026 to restructure. Without
getting into anything you don't want public, what does the next chapter of
Kintore look like?

4. How did you first find Bitcoin — was there a specific moment or person that
flipped the switch for you?

5. You came across BFTA through [Dion: confirm — Nostr, mutual contact, the
Jake Woodhouse Podcast?]. High-end residential design isn't the obvious crowd
for the Bitcoin-creative scene. What made you raise your hand?

6. Sound money and good design share a principle — scarcity by intention, not
by accident. As someone who builds rooms and objects meant to last decades,
does that parallel land for you?

7. You build for high-net-worth clients in Sydney's most expensive postcodes.
What are those clients actually saying — privately — about the Australian
dollar, real estate, and Bitcoin in 2026?

8. We're both creatives whose work is ultimately paid for by wealthy patrons —
your design clients, my dance audiences. Most Bitcoiners come from tech or
finance. What does our seat in that room let us see that they miss?

9. Your furniture brand Mat and Kin makes small-batch pieces in walnut and
cast bronze — made to outlast the buyer. Bitcoin's 21 million cap is the same
move at a different scale. Same conviction running both?

10. Interior design rewards patience — projects take a year, materials are
meant to read in decade three. Bitcoiners call that low time preference. Does
the way Bitcoin asks you to think about time feel native to what you already
do?

11. You've talked publicly about Bitcoin as a store of value alongside real
estate. As someone designing the houses people put their wealth into, what
does a Bitcoin-native client of the future actually look like?

12. Australian residential design lives on the back of leveraged real estate —
clients who borrowed to buy and renovate. If Bitcoin compresses the real
estate premium over the next decade, what changes for designers like you?

13. You've spoken publicly about "going where you're treated best." That's a
serious framework, especially for an Australian. What does living that out
actually look like for you and the studio?

14. Australia has a complicated relationship with money — banks closing
accounts, AUSTRAC scrutiny, restrictive crypto banking. What's it like
running a design business and holding Bitcoin in that environment?

15. If you could go back to early-career James — no portfolio, no leverage,
just starting out — what would you tell him about money and how to store it?

16. What would you say to a young Australian designer or maker today who
thinks Bitcoin is "a tech thing" that has nothing to do with their craft?

17. Five years from now — Kintore Design, Mat and Kin, James personally —
where do you want to be, and how does Bitcoin sit inside that picture?

18. Last question — we ask everyone this on Share Your Bitcoin Journey. If a
$500 to $2,000 Bitcoin micro-grant from BFTA landed in your wallet tomorrow,
what would it unlock for you as a creator?
